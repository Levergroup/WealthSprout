Why Do Parents Stress About This So Much?

Here's a question I get in my DMs at least twice a week: "Maya, how do I open a bank account for my child without messing it up?" And every single time, I want to reach through the screen and say — you are already doing better than most parents just by asking.

But I get the stress. I really do. When my oldest was seven, I stood in a bank lobby for twenty minutes holding a folder of documents I wasn't sure I needed, talking to a teller who seemed mildly annoyed, while my kid tried to steal the lollipops from the counter jar. I left without opening the account. Drove home. Googled everything. Tried again two weeks later. That's the version of this story nobody tells you — it's a little awkward the first time, and that's completely normal.

Why Opening a Bank Account for Your Kid Actually Matters

I'll be honest — I didn't think much about this until I was 34 and deep in paying off my own debt. I kept asking myself: why didn't anyone teach me this? And the answer is uncomfortable: most of us grew up in homes where money was either a secret or a source of stress, not a skill to practice.

Here's what the research actually says. According to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB), young adults who had savings accounts as children are three times more likely to have savings accounts as adults — and more likely to have higher balances. Three times. That's not a small effect. That's a trajectory-changing habit that starts with a $25 deposit and a kid watching their balance go from $25.00 to $25.47 after one month of interest.

The Next Gen Personal Finance (NGPF) organization reports that only 25 states currently require a personal finance course for high school graduation. Which means in most of the country, your kid could graduate high school without ever learning how compound interest works, what a checking account is, or why overdraft fees are basically a trap. That gap? It falls on us, the parents. And I know that feels like a lot — but opening a bank account is genuinely one of the easiest first steps you can take.

When your kid has their own account, they start seeing money as something real — not just numbers on a screen or cash that disappears. They learn to track it. They ask questions. My daughter once asked me why the bank "gave her money she didn't earn" (interest) and that conversation led to a 45-minute kitchen table talk about how banks work. That's the kind of financial education that sticks.

It also builds a credit history foundation. Some custodial accounts and teen checking accounts help establish banking relationships early, which matters when your kid is 18 and trying to rent an apartment or get their first credit card without you co-signing everything.

Mistakes Parents Make (That Are Totally Understandable)

None of these are judgment. I made at least three of them myself.

  1. Waiting until they're "old enough." There's no magic age. Kids as young as 5 can understand the concept of saving. Waiting until 16 means a decade of missed habit-building. If your kid is old enough to want something at the store, they're old enough to start learning about money.
  2. Opening an account but never talking about it. I see this one constantly. Parents open a savings account, deposit birthday money, and then… never mention it again. The account exists, but the learning doesn't happen. The account is a tool — the conversations are the education.
  3. Choosing the wrong account type. A regular adult savings account isn't designed for kids. Custodial accounts, joint accounts, and youth checking accounts have different rules, different fee structures, and different features. Picking the wrong one means your kid might face monthly fees that eat their savings, or no debit card access when they're ready for one.
  4. Ignoring fees. Some youth accounts charge monthly maintenance fees of $5–$10 if you don't meet minimum balance requirements. On a kid's account with $50 in it, that's a 10–20% fee. Always read the fine print. I didn't know to look for this until I was an adult and had already paid hundreds in unnecessary bank fees over the years.
  5. Not involving your kid in the process. Opening the account for them, without them, misses the whole point. Bring your kid to the bank or sit with them while you do it online. Let them answer questions when they can. Let them make the first deposit. That moment of "this is MY account" is where the mindset shift starts.

What Actually Works: Step-by-Step Guide

Okay, let's get into the actual how-to. I'm going to walk you through this the way I wish someone had walked me through it — no fluff, no vague advice, just the specific steps.

Step 1: Decide What Type of Account You Need

For kids under 18, you're looking at one of three options:

  • Custodial savings account: You (the parent) are the custodian. The money legally belongs to your kid, but you control it until they reach adulthood (18 or 21, depending on your state). Great for younger kids.
  • Joint checking or savings account: Both you and your kid are on the account. You both have full access. Good for teens who are starting to manage their own spending.
  • Youth-specific accounts: Many banks and credit unions offer accounts designed specifically for kids and teens, with no monthly fees, lower minimums, and built-in parental controls. These are often the best starting point.

If you want investing built in alongside banking, apps like Acorns Early combine a debit card with an investment account — which I didn't even know was possible until recently, and honestly wish I'd had for my kids sooner.

Step 2: Gather Your Documents

Whether you go in-person or online, you'll typically need:

  • Your government-issued ID (driver's license or passport)
  • Your child's Social Security number (SSN) — yes, they need one, and yes, this is normal
  • Your child's birth certificate (sometimes required, especially for younger kids)
  • An initial deposit — often as low as $1 to $25 depending on the bank

Pro tip: Call or check the bank's website before you go in person. Requirements vary and there's nothing worse than making the trip twice. (I made the trip twice.)

Step 3: Choose Your Bank or Credit Union

You don't have to use the same bank you use. In fact, sometimes it's better not to — a separate account at a different institution helps your kid see it as their money, not an extension of yours. Look for:

  • No monthly maintenance fees (or easy-to-waive ones)
  • No minimum balance requirements (or very low ones)
  • A mobile app your kid can actually use
  • Optional parental controls or spending alerts
  • FDIC or NCUA insurance (this protects deposits up to $250,000 — always confirm this)

Credit unions often have excellent youth accounts with fewer fees. Local community banks can be great too. For a deeper look at how interest works on these accounts, Investopedia has solid explainers you can read together with your teen.

Step 4: Open the Account — With Your Kid Present

This is the step most parents skip, and it's the most important one. Whether you're doing this online or in a branch, include your kid. Let them watch you fill out the form. Explain what you're doing in real time. "We're putting your name on this account — that means this money is yours." That sentence alone does more than a year of abstract money talks.

If you're going in person, call ahead and ask how long it takes (usually 20–30 minutes). Bring a snack. Bring patience. Bring the lollipop bribe if you need it — no judgment from me.

Step 5: Make the First Deposit Together

Even if it's $10 from their piggy bank, let your kid physically make the first deposit. Hand them the cash. Let them slide it across the counter or type in the amount online. Watch their face when the balance updates. That moment of "I have $10 in MY bank account" is genuinely magical and I'm not even being dramatic.

This is also a great time to connect the account to the 3-jar system if you're already using it — their "save" jar money now has a real home.

Step 6: Set Up a Routine Check-In

Once a month — maybe when you pay your own bills — sit down with your kid and look at the account together. Check the balance. Look at any transactions. Talk about what went in and what came out. This doesn't need to be a formal lesson. It can be five minutes at the kitchen table. Consistency is everything here. The habit of checking your account regularly is one of the most underrated financial skills an adult can have, and it starts here.

Age-by-Age Breakdown

Not every account type works for every age. Here's how I think about it:

Ages 5–8: The Foundation Years

At this age, your kid doesn't need a debit card — they need to understand that money is real, limited, and saveable. A basic custodial savings account works perfectly. Focus on deposits (birthday money, allowance, chore earnings) and watching the balance grow. The goal is the habit and the concept, not the amount. Even $5 a month is building something important. Pair it with physical money at home — a clear jar they can see filling up alongside the digital balance.

Also check out how to make money as a kid for age-appropriate earning ideas your 5–8 year old can actually do.

Ages 9–13: The Learning Years

Now your kid can handle more complexity. This is a great age to upgrade to a youth checking account that includes a debit card with parental controls. Let them make small purchases — a book, a game, a snack — and then review the transaction together. Talk about what the bank statement means. Introduce the idea of interest. My son was 11 when he first asked "wait, the bank pays ME?" and that question opened up a whole conversation about how banks use your money to make loans. He was genuinely fascinated. Kids this age are more ready for these concepts than we give them credit for.

Ages 14–18: The Practice Years

This is where it gets real. Teens this age may have part-time jobs, side hustles, or regular income from teen side hustles. They need a checking account they actively manage — with a debit card, mobile banking, and real spending decisions. Your role shifts from controller to coach. Set up direct deposit if they have a job. Talk about budgeting their paycheck. Introduce the concept of a Roth IRA (yes, even at 16 — if they have earned income, they can contribute). This is also the time to talk about what happens to the account when they turn 18 and it converts from custodial to fully theirs.

What Parents Are Actually Asking

These are real questions I see in parenting forums, Facebook groups, and my own inbox — answered the way I'd answer a friend over coffee.

"My kid is 10 and I still haven't done this. Am I behind?"

No. Full stop. The best time to open an account was five years ago; the second best time is this weekend. I didn't have a real handle on my own finances until I was 34, so I'm the last person who's going to make you feel bad about timing. What matters is that you're asking now. Open the account this week — even online takes 15 minutes — and you've already done more than most.

"Can I open a bank account for my child without them being there?"

Technically, yes — especially with custodial accounts, you can open one yourself since you're the legal custodian. But I'd really encourage you to involve your kid, even if it's just showing them the confirmation email and saying "look, this is your account." The account without the conversation is just a savings vehicle. The account WITH the conversation is a financial education. Bring them in whenever you can.

"What if we don't have much money to put in? Is it even worth it?"

Yes. Absolutely yes. The amount in the account matters way less than the habit of having one. I've seen parents open accounts with $5. The account exists, the habit starts, the conversations happen. Financial confidence isn't built by having a lot of money — it's built by having a relationship with money, whatever amount you have. Start with whatever you've got.

Frequently Asked Questions

What age can a child have a bank account?

There's no minimum age set by law in the U.S., but most banks require a parent or guardian to be a joint account holder or custodian until the child turns 18. Many banks offer youth accounts starting at age 6 or even younger. In practice, I'd say anytime after age 5 is a great time to start — even if it's just a simple savings account with a small balance.

Do I need my child's Social Security number to open an account?

Yes, in almost all cases. Federal law (specifically the Bank Secrecy Act) requires banks to collect a Social Security number for all account holders, including minors. If your child doesn't have one yet, you'll need to apply for one through the Social Security Administration before opening the account. It's a straightforward process — you can do it by mail or in person at your local SSA office.

What's the difference between a custodial account and a joint account for a child?

A custodial account is technically owned by the child but managed by a parent or guardian until the child reaches the age of majority (18 or 21, depending on the state). A joint account means both the parent and child are equal co-owners with full access. For younger kids, custodial accounts give parents more control. For teens who are actively managing their money, a joint account often makes more practical sense. Either way, the money habits you build around the account matter more than the account structure.

Are there bank accounts for kids with no fees?

Yes, and you should absolutely look for them. Many credit unions and online banks offer youth accounts with no monthly maintenance fees and no minimum balance requirements. Some popular options include accounts from Alliant Credit Union, Capital One MONEY, and Chase First Banking. Always read the fee schedule before opening — look specifically for monthly fees, overdraft fees, and ATM fees. A fee-free account is not hard to find if you know to look for it.

Can my child have their own debit card?

Yes — many youth checking accounts include a debit card for kids as young as 6 or 8. These cards typically come with parental controls: you can set spending limits, block certain merchant categories, get real-time notifications, and sometimes freeze the card instantly from an app. For younger kids, I'd recommend starting with a card that has tight controls and loosening them gradually as your kid demonstrates responsibility. It's one of the best ways to let them practice real spending decisions with real (but limited) consequences.

What happens to a custodial account when my child turns 18?

When your child reaches the age of majority in your state (18 in most states, 21 in a few), the custodial account legally transfers fully to them. They become the sole owner, and you no longer have control over it. This is actually a great conversation to have with your teen before it happens — talk about what's in the account, what it's for, and what their plan is. Some kids are ready for that responsibility; others need a little more runway. Either way, knowing it's coming helps everyone prepare.
