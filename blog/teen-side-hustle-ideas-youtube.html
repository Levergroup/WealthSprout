Teen Side Hustle Ideas: How to Actually Make Money on YouTube

Maya Hartwell Parent-Tested ✓

Former math teacher · Mom of two · Editor, WealthSprout

📅 November 28, 2026 · Updated November 28, 2026 · 10 min read

By Maya Hartwell

Let me guess: you've been told you're "too young" to get a real job, or the jobs available to you pay $8/hour folding clothes at the mall. Meanwhile, you're watching creators your age pull in actual money talking about video games, thrifting, or literally just their morning routine.

Here's what I wish someone had told me at 16: YouTube isn't just entertainment. It's one of the few side hustles where your age doesn't matter, your location doesn't matter, and you don't need anyone's permission to start.

This guide breaks down exactly how to turn YouTube into a real side hustle, not just a daydream.

The Problem: Why Most Teens Struggle to Make Money Online

The internet is full of "make money as a teen!" advice that falls into two categories: completely vague ("start a business!") or borderline scammy ("make $500 a day with this one trick!").

The real problems teens face:

  • Age restrictions everywhere. Most freelance platforms, payment processors, and monetization programs require you to be 18. YouTube's Partner Program? Also 18.
  • No one takes you seriously. Try pitching a business idea as a 15-year-old and watch adults smile and nod like you just told them you want to be an astronaut.
  • The advice assumes you have money to start. "Invest in good equipment!" Cool, with what money?
  • School takes up your entire day. You're not available for a regular job even if you wanted one.

YouTube actually solves most of these problems, but not in the way most articles explain it.

The Stats: What's Actually Happening with Teen Creators

Let's talk real numbers, because the YouTube success stories you see are misleading.

According to Investopedia's breakdown of YouTube earnings, the average payment per 1,000 views (called CPM) ranges from $3 to $5. That means:

  • 10,000 views = roughly $30-50
  • 100,000 views = roughly $300-500
  • 1 million views = roughly $3,000-5,000

But here's what matters more: you don't need millions of views to make meaningful money as a teen. A channel getting 50,000 views per month could earn $150-250/month. That's more than most part-time jobs pay after taxes, and you're working on your own schedule.

The other stat that matters: YouTube has 2.7 billion users. The platform isn't saturated; it's enormous. There's room for your specific thing.

The Solution: YouTube as a Real Side Hustle for Teens

YouTube works as a teen side hustle for three specific reasons:

1. You can start with a phone. That's it. The camera you already have is good enough. The first 50 videos from successful creators all look terrible. You're not behind.

2. You work when school isn't happening. Film on weekends, edit after homework, post on your schedule. No manager texting you to come in on Saturday.

3. It scales without you. A video you post today can still be earning money six months from now. That's not true of hourly work.

The strategy that actually works: pick something you already do or know, and document it. Not perform it, document it.

  • You thrift? Show your finds and what you paid.
  • You play Minecraft? Record your building process.
  • You're trying to save money? Film your weekly budget check-ins.
  • You're learning to cook? Show what you're making.

The best teen YouTube channels aren't trying to be Mr. Beast. They're showing real life with good pacing.

How to Start a YouTube Side Hustle (Step-by-Step)

Step 1: Pick your one thing (this weekend)

Not five things. One. What do you already spend time on that you could film? Pick the thing that you'd do anyway even if nobody watched.

Step 2: Study three channels in that niche (1 hour)

Find three channels doing something similar. Watch their most popular video. Notice:

  • How long is it? (Probably 8-15 minutes)
  • How do they start? (First 10 seconds matter most)
  • What's the pacing? (Likely faster than you think)
  • What's in the thumbnail? (Faces, text, bright colors)

You're not copying. You're learning the pattern.

Step 3: Make your first video this week (seriously)

Film it on your phone. Use natural light from a window. Keep it under 10 minutes. The goal is to finish it and post it, not to make it perfect.

Use CapCut or iMovie to edit (both free). Cut out the boring parts. That's 90% of editing.

Step 4: Post consistently (pick a schedule you can actually keep)

Once a week is realistic. Every two weeks is fine. Three times a week is ambitious. The key: pick a schedule and keep it for at least three months. YouTube rewards consistency more than quality at the start.

Step 5: Learn from your analytics (after you have 10 videos)

YouTube Studio shows you exactly what's working. Which videos do people click on? Where do they stop watching? Make more of what works.

What School Doesn't Teach About Making Money on YouTube

Your school probably taught you how to analyze a novel but not how to analyze why people click on videos. Here's what actually matters:

Thumbnails are worth more than fancy editing. A clear, bright thumbnail with readable text gets more clicks than a beautifully edited video with a boring thumbnail. Make your thumbnail in Canva (free) before you even start filming. If you can't make a compelling thumbnail, the video idea might not be strong enough.

The first 10 seconds determine everything. If viewers leave in the first 30 seconds, YouTube stops recommending your video. Start with the payoff, not the setup. "Here's the finished outfit" comes before "So I went thrifting last week..."

YouTube is a search engine. People type in questions. Your video titles should answer those questions. "How I saved $500 in 2 months as a 16-year-old" performs better than "My savings journey."

Community matters more than subscribers. Ten people who comment on every video are worth more than 1,000 ghost followers. Respond to every comment in your first year. Build the audience before you need them.

Age-by-Age: What's Realistic at 13, 15, and 17

At 13-14:

You can start a channel and build an audience, but you can't monetize it yourself yet (AdSense requires 18). The play here: build now, monetize later. Or have a parent set up the AdSense account with you.

Realistic goal: 100 subscribers in 6 months, learning how to make videos people want to watch.

Best niches: Gaming, Roblox/Minecraft content, school hacks, room tours, "a day in the life," beginner tutorials for things you're learning.

At 15-16:

Same monetization situation, but you have more independence to film and edit. You're also old enough that brands might send you free products to review (not cash, but still valuable).

Realistic goal: 500-1,000 subscribers in 6 months if you're consistent, with a parent-managed AdSense account earning $50-200/month once you hit monetization requirements.

Best niches: Thrifting/fashion, study tips, how you save money, budget challenges, cooking on a budget, beginner fitness, art processes.

At 17-18:

If you're 18, you can handle your own AdSense. At 17, you're one year away and should be building hard.

Realistic goal: 1,000+ subscribers in 6 months (the threshold for monetization, along with 4,000 watch hours). First paycheck 6-12 months after starting if you're strategic.

Best niches: Everything above, plus college prep, scholarship tips, first job experiences, learning to invest, moving out preparation, adulting content.

Common Mistakes Teens Make (and How to Avoid Them)

Mistake 1: Waiting until everything is perfect

Your first 20 videos will be rough. Post them anyway. You can't learn without feedback, and feedback only comes after you publish.

Mistake 2: Copying exactly what big creators do

Mr. Beast has a team of 50 people. You have a phone and a weekend. Make content that fits your resources. Authenticity beats production value for small channels.

Mistake 3: Giving up after one month

Your first month will probably get you 20 subscribers and 500 views total. That's normal. YouTube growth isn't linear. Months 1-3 feel like nothing's happening. Months 4-6 is when you start seeing traction if you've been consistent.

Mistake 4: Ignoring the business side

YouTube is a business, even if it's a side hustle. Track your expenses (even if it's just $0 right now). Save screenshots of your analytics. Document your growth. This matters for taxes later and for understanding what works.

Learn more about the money management side in our guide on how to save money as a teenager.

Mistake 5: Not treating it like a real side hustle

If you post randomly when you feel like it, you'll get random results. Set a schedule. Block out production time. Treat it like you would a part-time job, even before you're making money.

What People Are Actually Wondering

This question comes up constantly: "Being a teenager under 18, can I earn money through YouTube? I want to start a channel but I'm worried about the AdSense age requirement."

Yes, you can earn money under 18, but your parent needs to set up and manage the AdSense account. You create the content, they handle the payment side until you're 18. It's a partnership that also keeps things legal and helps with taxes (which yes, you'll need to pay on YouTube income).

The good news: building an audience before you can monetize means you're ready to earn immediately once you hit the requirements. Start now, monetize later.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much money can a teen realistically make on YouTube?

In your first year, $0-500 total is realistic if you're consistent. By year two, teens with 5,000-10,000 subscribers typically earn $100-500/month. The top 5% of teen creators make $1,000+/month, but they're usually posting multiple times per week and have been at it for 2+ years. Your mileage varies wildly based on niche and consistency.

Do I need expensive equipment to start a YouTube channel?

No. Your smartphone is enough. If your phone is from the last 5 years, it probably shoots better video than the cameras YouTubers were using in 2015. Add a $15 ring light from Amazon if your room is dark, but even that's optional. Natural window light is free and looks great.

How many subscribers do you need to make money on YouTube?

You need 1,000 subscribers AND 4,000 watch hours in the past 12 months to join the YouTube Partner Program and earn ad revenue. For most consistent creators, this takes 6-18 months. But you can earn money before that through affiliate links, selling your own products, or sponsorships if you have an engaged audience.

Can I get in trouble at school for having a YouTube channel?

Not unless you're filming during school hours or posting content that violates your school's code of conduct. Keep school and YouTube separate. Don't film on campus without permission, don't talk about teachers or classmates, and you're fine. Some schools even celebrate student creators.

What if I'm too nervous to show my face on camera?

Faceless channels work. Screen recording tutorials, voiceover content, hand-only videos (cooking, art, crafts), gaming with just your voice. Some of the most successful channels never show the creator's face. It's harder to build connection without showing yourself, but absolutely possible.

How do I deal with negative comments as a teen creator?

Turn off comments until you have 100 subscribers and a supportive core audience. When you turn them back on, delete and block anyone who's just being mean (not critical, mean—there's a difference). Never engage with negative comments. Responding gives them attention and makes you feel worse. The CFPB has resources on digital literacy that include managing your online presence safely.
