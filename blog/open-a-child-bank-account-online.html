But here's what really got me: research from Cambridge University found that money habits are largely set by age 7. Age. Seven.

My oldest was 8 when I learned this, and I immediately felt behind. If habits form that early, and most schools don't teach personal finance (I taught middle school math for a decade — trust me, they don't), then the window for parents to step in is smaller than we think.

Opening a bank account for your kid isn't just about having a place to stash birthday money. It's one of the most concrete ways to make money real to a child. They can see numbers grow. They can watch interest appear like magic. They start to understand that money isn't just something you hand over — it's something you manage.

The good news? You can open a child bank account online in about 15 minutes. I timed myself last month when I helped my sister set one up for my nephew. Fourteen minutes, including the time she spent looking for his Social Security card.

Why Most Parents Keep Putting This Off

I talk to a lot of parents about money. And when I ask why they haven't opened a bank account for their kid yet, I hear the same things:

"I don't know which bank to choose." Fair. There are approximately one million options, and they all seem the same until you read the fine print about fees.

"I assumed I had to go in person." This was true for a long time! But most banks — especially online banks and credit unions — now let you complete the entire process from your couch.

"I'm not sure my kid is old enough." There's no legal minimum age. Some parents open accounts when their babies are born. For teaching purposes, I think 5-6 is a sweet spot because your kid can actually participate in watching their balance.

"We just use a piggy bank." Nothing wrong with piggy banks! But a piggy bank can't show your kid how interest works. It can't send them a statement. It can't grow. At some point, upgrading from physical cash to a real account makes the lessons stick differently.

The real issue? It feels like one more thing on the list. And when something doesn't feel urgent, it slides to next week. Then next month. Then suddenly your kid is 12 and has never seen a bank statement.

How to Open a Child Bank Account Online (Step-by-Step)

Here's exactly how to open a child bank account online, broken down so you can knock it out during naptime or after bedtime.

Step 1: Gather Your Documents (5 minutes)

Before you start clicking around bank websites, grab these:

  • Your government-issued ID — driver's license or passport
  • Your Social Security number
  • Your child's Social Security number — this is the one people often have to hunt for
  • Your child's birth certificate — some banks require this, many don't
  • Proof of your address — utility bill, bank statement, anything recent

Pro tip: take a photo of your kid's Social Security card and store it somewhere secure (password-protected folder, secure notes app). You'll need this number more than you'd expect.

Step 2: Choose Your Bank Type

Traditional banks (Chase, Bank of America, Wells Fargo) — great if you already bank there. Easy to link your account to your kid's. Downsides: often lower interest rates, may have more fees.

Online banks (Ally, Capital One 360, Discover) — typically higher interest rates, lower or no fees. My kids' accounts are at an online bank because the interest rate was 10x higher than our local branch.

Credit unions — often the best rates and most kid-friendly accounts. May require membership (usually easy to qualify). Many have excellent financial education programs built in.

For a deeper dive on which type might work best for your family, check out our complete guide to kids' bank accounts.

Step 3: Decide on Account Type

Savings account: best starting point for kids under 10. Teaches the "watch it grow" concept. Limited transactions — which is actually good, reinforcing that this money isn't for spending.

Checking account with debit card: better for kids 11+ who are ready to practice spending decisions. Consider this as a second account once they've mastered savings basics.

Custodial account: you're the legal owner until your kid turns 18 (or 21 in some states). This is the most common type and gives you full control.

For most families with kids 5-13, I recommend: start with a custodial savings account. Add a checking account with a debit card when they're 11-13 and ready for more responsibility.

Step 4: Complete the Online Application (10-15 minutes)

Navigate to your chosen bank's website and look for "Open an Account" — then find options for minor/youth/kids accounts. Here's what you'll typically fill out:

  1. Your information first — name, address, SSN, date of birth, employment info
  2. Your child's information — name, SSN, date of birth
  3. Account preferences — type of account, paperless statements (yes please)
  4. Initial deposit amount — some banks require $0, others want $25-$100
  5. Verification — some banks verify instantly, others may ask follow-up questions

If you get stuck, most banks have a phone number you can call to complete the process with a human. Don't let one confusing field derail you.

Step 5: Make the First Deposit Together

Have your kid watch you transfer the money. Show them the account balance go from $0 to $10. Explain that this is their money now, and the bank is keeping it safe.

My 9-year-old still remembers her first deposit. It was $15 from her piggy bank, and she asked me approximately 47 questions about where the money "goes."

Step 6: Set Up Access

Most banks let you create login credentials for your child (supervised access), set up alerts for deposits and withdrawals, enable automatic transfers for allowance, and order a debit card if it's a checking account.

I set up a text alert that goes to both me and my kid whenever there's activity on her account. She loves getting the "deposit received" notifications.

What Parents Get Wrong About Kids' Bank Accounts

After talking with hundreds of parents and making some of these mistakes myself, here's what trips people up:

Mistake #1: Choosing Based on the Free Toy

Some banks give kids a stuffed animal or branded piggy bank when they open an account. Cute? Sure. But don't let a $5 toy sway you toward an account with monthly fees that'll cost you $60/year.

Mistake #2: Not Checking the Fee Structure

Always look for: no monthly maintenance fees, no minimum balance requirements, no inactivity fees, and no paper statement charges. If you see any of these for a kids' account, walk away.

Mistake #3: Opening It and Forgetting About It

The account itself won't teach your kid anything. The value comes from talking about it. Check the balance together. Celebrate when interest posts. Discuss goals. If you open an account and never mention it again, you've just created a dusty savings vehicle, not a financial education tool.

Mistake #4: Starting Too Big

You don't need to fund the account with $500. Starting small ($10-$25) can be better because your child gets to experience the thrill of watching it grow from a small number. When my daughter hit $100, she literally screamed. That wouldn't have happened if we'd started at $200.

Mistake #5: Not Explaining What Interest Is

When that first interest payment shows up (even if it's $0.03), use it. "See that? The bank paid you three cents just for keeping your money here. That's called interest. The more you save, the more they'll pay you." This is the seed of understanding compound interest — one of the most powerful concepts in personal finance.

Quick Wins You Can Do This Week

Today: find your child's Social Security card or number. That's usually the thing that slows parents down. If you can't find it, you can request a replacement at ssa.gov.

Tomorrow: pick two banks to compare. Check if they offer kids' accounts online, what the minimum deposit is, and whether there are fees. Compare one traditional bank and one online bank or credit union.

This weekend: talk to your kid about it. "We're going to open a bank account for you. Your money will be kept somewhere super safe, and it'll even grow a little bit over time." Gauge their questions and excitement level.

Next week: block 20 minutes, open the account, make the first deposit together, and celebrate.

Once the account is open, connect it to their money learning. If your family uses the 3-jar system, the bank account becomes the digital version of the "save" jar. If your kid is starting to earn their own money, our guide on how to make money as a kid pairs perfectly with their new account.

What Parents Are Actually Asking

I spend a lot of time reading what parents are genuinely confused about. Here's what's coming up again and again:

"Looking to open a bank account for my 7 year old so they have an understanding of money. Credit unions I looked up want a lot of info up front. Do I really have to give them this much personal info for my kid?"

— via Reddit

I totally get the hesitation — handing over your 7-year-old's Social Security number feels weird. But this is actually required by federal law (the Patriot Act) to prevent fraud. Every legitimate bank will ask for it. The good news: banks have serious security requirements, so your kid's info is typically safer there than on the average app.

"Any considerations when opening a bank account for my child? Thinking of putting some money away for her in an account she won't have access to."

— via Reddit

If you want to save money for your child without them having access, a custodial account (UTMA) keeps you in control until they're 18-21. But if the goal is teaching — not just saving — consider letting them see the account even if they can't withdraw. Watching money grow teaches way more than finding out about it later.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I open a bank account online for my child?

Yes. Most major banks and credit unions now let you open a child bank account online in 10-15 minutes. You'll need your ID, Social Security numbers for both you and your kid, and proof of address.

What age can a child have their own bank account?

There's no federal minimum age. For practical teaching purposes, ages 5-6 is when many parents start because kids can understand basic counting and saving concepts. At 13+, some banks offer teen checking with debit cards.

What documents do I need to open a child bank account online?

Your government-issued ID, your child's Social Security number, your child's birth certificate or Social Security card (sometimes), proof of your address, and your own Social Security number. Having these ready makes the application take under 15 minutes.

Are there fees for kids' bank accounts?

Many banks waive monthly fees for accounts held by minors. Look for no minimum balance requirements and no monthly maintenance fees. Credit unions especially tend to offer fee-free kids' accounts.

Should I get my child a savings account or checking account?

For kids under 10, start with a savings account. It reinforces the concept of money growing over time. Once your kid is 11-13 and ready to manage spending, add a checking account with a debit card. Many families maintain both — savings for goals, checking for allowance and spending.

Your Next Step

Reading about opening a bank account for your kid is useful, but actually opening one is what matters. And I know from experience that the gap between "I should do this" and "I did this" can stretch for months if there's no clear next step.

So here's yours: in the next 48 hours, find your child's Social Security number. That's it. Put it somewhere you can access it, and you've removed the biggest friction point. The actual application is the easy part.

You're already ahead just by thinking about this. Most parents don't. Your kid is lucky to have someone in their corner who cares about setting them up early.
