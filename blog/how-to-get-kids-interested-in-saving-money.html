Why Most Kids Don't Save (And It's Not What You Think)

Only 21% of kids ages 8-14 save most of their money, according to T. Rowe Price's 2023 Parents, Kids & Money Survey. That means nearly 8 out of 10 kids spend everything they get.

Here's what shocked me even more: It's not because kids are bad with money or too young to understand. The same survey found that 61% of kids said they want to save for something specific—but they're not actually doing it.

The gap between wanting to save and actually saving? That's where we as parents need to show up differently.

My 9-year-old told me last year she wanted to save for a $60 art set. Three weeks later, she'd spent her entire birthday money on fidget toys. That moment taught me wanting to save and knowing HOW to make it interesting are completely different skills.

The Real Issue: Saving Is Invisible and Boring

Let's be honest: saving money is the broccoli of financial skills. It's good for you, everyone says you should do it, and it delivers exactly zero dopamine hits.

Spending gives kids instant feedback. They hand over money, they get a toy, they feel good. Saving? They put money in a jar and... nothing happens. No lights, no sounds, no new thing in their hands.

The second problem is that most savings goals for kids are either too vague ("save for college") or too distant (a bike that costs $200 when they earn $5 a week). That's 40 weeks of delayed gratification for a 10-year-old. In kid-time, that's basically forever.

And here's the part I didn't understand until I was 34: We're asking kids to do something most adults struggle with. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau reports that 40% of American adults don't have $400 for an emergency. If we can't make saving feel rewarding, why would kids choose it?

The real issue isn't that kids don't understand saving. It's that we haven't made it visible, exciting, or rewarding enough to compete with spending.

Step-by-Step: Making Saving Actually Interesting

Step 1: Make Savings Visible and Physical

Forget the piggy bank where money disappears into darkness. Your kid needs to SEE their progress.

Use a clear jar or container for their savings. Every time they add money, they can literally watch the pile grow. My daughter's motivation jumped when we switched from a ceramic pig to a clear mason jar—she could see her $20 in ones and fives getting taller.

If your kid is 9 or older and saving for something bigger, create a visual tracker. Draw a thermometer on paper, use a chart with stickers, or print a progress bar they color in. The three-jar system works great here because the "Save" jar has a specific job kids can track.

Step 2: Create Short-Term Savings Wins

Start with goals your kid can reach in 2-4 weeks maximum. Not months. Weeks.

If your 7-year-old gets $3 a week allowance, their first savings goal should be something around $9-12. That's 3-4 weeks of saving. Totally doable, and they get that rewarding feeling before they lose interest.

After they hit that first goal, increase the next one slightly. Maybe $15, then $25. You're building the muscle memory of "I saved, I reached my goal, this feels good."

Step 3: Add a Matching System

This is the game-changer most parents don't know about. Offer to match your kid's savings at a specific ratio for specific goals.

For example: "For every $5 you save toward that skateboard, I'll add $2." Suddenly they're not saving $60 alone—they're saving $43 and you're contributing $17. The timeline shrinks, the goal feels achievable, and they're learning that saving can actually multiply their money.

This mirrors how employer 401(k) matching works, which I explain more on our Money Moves page. You're teaching compound interest concepts without using those words.

Step 4: Create Savings Milestones with Mini-Rewards

Break bigger goals into chunks with small celebrations along the way.

If your kid is saving $40 for a video game, celebrate at $10 (maybe they pick the movie for family night), $20 (they get to stay up 15 minutes later one night), and $30 (you add $2 bonus). The final goal at $40 is the game itself.

The milestones make the waiting period feel less endless. According to research from Next Gen Personal Finance, breaking goals into smaller pieces increases follow-through rates significantly—even for adults.

Step 5: Let Them Experience the Win

When your kid reaches their savings goal, make the purchase memorable. Go to the store together, let them hand over their saved money, take a photo.

This is the moment that cements the whole lesson: "I wanted something, I saved for it, I got it with my own money." That feeling is powerful.

One parent I know created a "Savings Success" photo album where they documented each item their son bought with saved money. At 12, he still looks back at it with pride.

What People Get Wrong About Teaching Kids to Save

Mistake #1: Starting with Long-Term, Abstract Goals

"Save for college" means nothing to a 7-year-old. Even "save for the future" is too vague.

Start with concrete, short-term goals they can visualize: a specific Lego set, a particular book, a game they've been asking about. Once they've experienced the save-and-win cycle a few times, you can introduce longer-term goals.

Mistake #2: Making Saving Feel Like Punishment

I hear parents say things like "You can't have that now, you need to save your money." The message kids hear: saving is the thing stopping me from getting what I want.

Flip it: "You want that $30 toy? Cool. Let's figure out how you can save up for it." Now saving is the tool that gets them what they want, not the barrier.

Mistake #3: No Interest or Growth

Money sitting in a jar doesn't grow. It's boring and it doesn't teach kids that savings can work for you.

Add interest manually. Every month, add 5-10% to whatever they've saved. If they have $20 in savings, you add $1-2. Keep it simple, but make it visible. Write "Interest: +$1.50" on a slip of paper and let them add it to their jar.

This is the foundation for understanding how compound interest works in real savings accounts later.

Mistake #4: Forcing Every Dollar Into Savings

If 100% of their money must be saved, kids feel broke all the time. That builds resentment, not good habits.

Use a split system: maybe 30% for saving, 60% for spending, 10% for giving or sharing. They get to enjoy some money now while building their savings muscle. The three-jar system is perfect for this.

Mistake #5: Talking About Saving But Not Modeling It

Kids watch everything we do. If we're constantly making impulse purchases but telling them to save, the message doesn't land.

Talk out loud about your own saving decisions: "I really want to buy this, but I'm saving for our vacation, so I'm going to wait." Let them see you choose delayed gratification sometimes too.

Quick Wins You Can Try Today

You don't need a complex system to start. Here are five things you can do this afternoon:

Quick Win #1: Switch to a clear container for savings right now. Move their money from wherever it's hiding into a jar they can see. Label it with their current savings goal.

Quick Win #2: Ask your kid what they want to save for and write it down together. If they say something expensive, help them pick a smaller "first goal" they can hit in 3-4 weeks. Make it specific: not "a toy" but "the blue race car at Target that costs $12."

Quick Win #3: Create a one-page savings tracker tonight. Draw 10-20 boxes on paper, write the goal at the top, and let them color one box for each dollar (or $5, depending on their goal size). Tape it to their bedroom wall.

Quick Win #4: Offer a one-time match today. "I'll add $3 to whatever you put in your savings jar this week." Watch how motivated they suddenly become.

Quick Win #5: Add "interest" to their current savings at dinner tonight. Even if it's just $1, make a show of it. Explain that saved money can grow. Let them add it to their jar themselves.

What Parents Are Actually Asking

I spend way too much time on parenting Reddit threads (it's where I get my best ideas). Here are two questions that come up constantly:

"How do you teach your kids about money without making them anxious about it?"

— r/financialindependence

This is such a smart question because it gets at a real tension: we want kids to respect money without fearing it.

The answer is focusing on what money enables, not what happens without it. Instead of "we can't afford that," try "we're choosing to save our money for the camping trip." Instead of "money is tight," try "we have $20 for fun stuff this week—what should we do with it?"

Make money conversations about choices and possibilities, not scarcity and stress. Let savings be about reaching goals, not avoiding disasters.

"How did you teach your kids the value of money?"

— r/Parenting

The "value" of money is abstract until kids experience trade-offs themselves.

The best teaching tool is letting them make spending decisions with their own money and live with the results. When my daughter spent all her money on fidget toys, she felt the sting of not having enough for the art set two weeks later. I didn't lecture—I just said, "Yeah, that's frustrating. What could you do differently next time?"

She learned more from that $15 mistake than from a hundred conversations about "spending wisely." Let them experience small financial consequences now while the stakes are low.

Common Questions About Getting Kids Interested in Saving

What age should I start teaching my kid to save money?

You can start as early as age 5 with very simple concepts. At this age, use a clear jar and help them save for something small they can reach in 1-2 weeks, like a $6 toy.

Ages 7-9 are the sweet spot for introducing the concept of saving for bigger things and using a percentage-based system like save-spend-share. By age 10-12, most kids can handle longer savings timelines (4-8 weeks) and understand basic interest concepts.

How much should my kid save from their allowance?

There's no magic number, but 20-30% is a solid starting point that feels doable without making them feel broke.

If your kid gets $10 a week, that's $2-3 going into savings, $6-7 for spending, and maybe $1 for giving. Adjust based on their age and goals—younger kids might do better with 20%, older kids might choose to save 40% if they're working toward something specific.

The key is they need enough spending money to feel some freedom, or the whole system feels like punishment.

My kid keeps "borrowing" from their savings jar. How do I stop this?

First, this is super common and not a character flaw. It means their savings goal is probably too far away or too vague.

Shorten the timeline. Pick a smaller goal they can hit in 2-3 weeks instead of 2-3 months. Make sure they have enough spending money that they're not constantly feeling deprived.

If they're older (9+), you can also introduce a "savings contract" where they write down their goal and agree not to touch that jar until they reach it. Some kids respond well to that formality.

Should I pay my kid interest on their savings?

Yes, absolutely. Even if it's just 5-10% monthly, paying interest makes savings feel active instead of passive.

Calculate it simply: if they have $20 saved at the end of the month, add $1-2 in "interest." Let them add it to their jar themselves. This teaches them that saved money can grow, which is the foundation for understanding real savings accounts, CDs, and eventually investing.

Once they're 10-12 and have built the saving habit, you can transition them to a real kids' savings account that pays actual interest.

What if my kid reaches their savings goal and loses motivation?

This is actually a great problem to have — it means the system worked. Celebrate the win first, then immediately set the next goal together.

Ask your kid what they want to save for next. Let them pick something that excites them. The momentum from reaching one goal is the best fuel for starting the next one. Kids who experience the satisfaction of reaching a savings goal are far more likely to keep saving — the habit builds on itself.
