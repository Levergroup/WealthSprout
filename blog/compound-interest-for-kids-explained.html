If your kid invested $1,000 at age 10 and never touched it, it could be worth over $17,000 by the time they retire — without adding a single extra dollar. That's not a typo. That's compound interest doing what it does best: turning small, early money into something that feels almost unfair.

Most parents I talk to know compound interest is "good" in a vague sort of way. But the specifics? The actual mechanics? I didn't fully get it until I was 34, sitting across from a financial advisor, genuinely embarrassed. The Problem Most Parents Don't See

Here's the thing nobody tells you: the biggest threat to your kid's financial future isn't bad spending habits. It's starting late. Every year your kid doesn't have money working for them is a year they'll never get back — and in compound interest math, those early years are worth the most.

My 9-year-old asked me last week why we couldn't just "put money in the bank and get rich." Honestly? It opened up the best conversation we've had about money. Because she was almost right — the problem is most savings accounts pay next to nothing (we're talking 0.01% at big banks), so the money just sits there, barely breathing.

Parents often assume school is covering this. It's not. According to Next Gen Personal Finance, only 25 states require a personal finance course to graduate high school. And even those courses often skim compound interest in a single lesson. Your kid is on their own — unless you step in.

The Numbers That Should Shock You

Let's get specific, because vague feels forgettable and specific changes behavior. Here are the numbers I wish someone had put in front of me at 25 — let alone at 10.

  • Start at 10 vs. start at 20: A kid who invests $1,000 at age 10 (7% average annual return, S&P 500 historical average) ends up with ~$29,500 at age 65. A kid who starts the same investment at 20? About $14,970. Same money. Ten-year head start doubles the outcome.
  • The $50/month example: If your kid puts $50/month into a custodial Roth IRA starting at 13, by age 65 they'd have roughly $277,000 — having contributed only $31,200 themselves. The rest? Pure compounding.
  • Waiting costs real dollars: According to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, Americans who start saving in their 30s instead of their 20s need to save nearly twice as much per month to reach the same goal.

These aren't abstract numbers. These are the stakes. And the reason I'm not even a little embarrassed to talk about this on a Tuesday afternoon in a carpool line.

The Simple Solution: Compound Interest

Compound interest is your kid's best financial friend — and it works whether they're paying attention or not. Once the money is in the right account, it grows while they sleep, while they're at soccer practice, while they're arguing about what to watch on Netflix.

The short version: compound interest means you earn interest on your interest. Not just on the original amount you put in — on everything that's already accumulated. It snowballs. Slowly at first, then faster and faster the longer it runs.

The key lever is time. Not income. Not some special trick. Just starting early and leaving the money alone. That's it. Which means a 10-year-old with $500 has a genuine, measurable advantage over a 30-year-old with $5,000 — at least in terms of how long compounding has to work. Help your kid earn that first $500 here.

How Compound Interest Actually Works (Step by Step)

Let's break this down the way I explain it to my own kids — no jargon, no hand-waving.

Step 1: You start with a principal. Say your kid puts $100 into an account that earns 10% per year (we're using round numbers to make the math clean — real returns vary).

Step 2: Year one, they earn interest. 10% of $100 = $10. Now they have $110.

Step 3: Year two, the interest earns interest. Now it's 10% of $110 — not $100. That's $11. They now have $121.

Step 4: Repeat. Forever. Every year, the base gets bigger. The interest gets bigger. The snowball rolls faster.

Here's a simple table to show your kid what that $100 looks like over time at 10%:

  • Year 1: $110
  • Year 5: $161
  • Year 10: $259
  • Year 20: $672
  • Year 30: $1,745

That last number — $1,745 from a $100 starting point — is what makes my older kid's eyes go wide every time. No extra deposits. No lottery. Just time.

For a deeper dive into the formula behind this, Investopedia's compound interest explainer is legitimately one of the clearest I've found for older kids (12+) who want to see the actual math.

What School Never Taught Your Kid About Money

I taught middle school math for six years. I loved it. And I never once taught compound interest in a way that connected to real life — because the curriculum didn't ask me to. We did percentages. We did interest rate problems as word problems on a worksheet. Nobody said: "This is the thing that will either make you wealthy or leave you broke depending on which side of it you're on."

Here's what school skips entirely:

  • Compound interest works against you too. Credit card debt compounds just like savings — except it's compounding at 20–29% APR in the wrong direction. Teaching your kid the upside without the downside is only half the lesson.
  • Index funds are how most people actually access compound growth. Putting money in a standard savings account at 0.01% isn't compounding in any meaningful way. A custodial account invested in something like VOO (Vanguard S&P 500 ETF) is closer to the real thing.
  • The Roth IRA is one of the best tools a teenager can have. If your kid has earned income (from a job, lawn mowing, babysitting — see our guide on how kids can make real money), they can open a custodial Roth IRA. Contributions grow tax-free. Forever. That's an enormous advantage school never mentions.
  • Starting the conversation at home is the curriculum. Kids who talk about money at home make better financial decisions as adults. That's not my opinion — it's what the research from CFPB consistently shows. You don't need to be an expert. You just need to not avoid it.

The 3-jar system we teach in Money Moves is a great starting framework before you get into compound interest — it builds the habit of saving before you explain why saving works so hard for you.

How to Get Your Kid Started Today

Concrete steps only. Here's exactly what I'd do — and what I've done with my own kids.

Ages 9–11: Make it visual with a "doubling demo." Grab a piece of paper. Start with 1 penny and double it every day for 30 days on paper. By day 30 they'd have over $5 million. It's absurd. It's memorable. It makes the point that doubling (compounding) over time is more powerful than adding.

Ages 11–13: Open a high-yield savings account together. Marcus by Goldman Sachs and Ally both currently offer 4%+ APY. It's not the stock market, but it's real compound interest they can watch grow in a real account. Let them check the balance monthly and notice the interest line.

If your kid has earned income: Open a custodial Roth IRA. Fidelity's Youth Account is free to open and has no minimums. Vanguard's custodial Roth IRA is another solid option. Put $25/month into a total market index fund like FSKAX or VTI and leave it alone. Revisit it every birthday.

Talk about it like it's normal — because it should be. At dinner. In the car. When the topic comes up naturally. Not as a lecture. Just: "Hey, remember that $50 we put in last month? It made $1.40 just sitting there." That's the whole lesson, delivered in 10 seconds.

I spend a lot of time in personal finance communities online, and the questions parents ask about compound interest tend to cluster around the same two things: how to explain it simply, and how to make it real. Here are two that I think are worth addressing directly.

The top-voted answer in that thread used a snowball rolling down a hill — it picks up more snow the bigger it gets, and bigger snowballs pick up snow faster. That's genuinely the best one-sentence explanation I've seen. I use it myself. The key is the visual: it grows slowly, then shockingly fast.

The consistent theme: kids get it faster than we expect when we show them their own money growing. Not a hypothetical. Their actual dollars.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is compound interest in simple terms for kids?

Compound interest means you earn interest on your interest — not just on the money you originally put in. So your money grows faster the longer it sits, because each year's growth adds to the base that earns next year's growth. Think of it as a snowball that picks up more snow the bigger it gets.

At what age should I teach my kid about compound interest?

Age 9 or 10 is a solid starting point for the concept — they can do the basic math and understand "more money makes more money." But you can introduce the snowball idea even younger with physical coins. The earlier the better, since the whole lesson is about time being your biggest advantage.

What's the best way to show compound interest to a child?

Open a real account with real money and let them track it. A high-yield savings account at Ally or Marcus shows interest deposits monthly — your kid can literally watch their money grow. For older kids (12+), a spreadsheet where they project their balance over 10, 20, and 30 years tends to land hard.

How much money does compound interest actually make over time?

It depends on the rate and the time — but the numbers get big fast. A single $1,000 investment at age 10, earning 7% annually, grows to roughly $29,500 by age 65 with no additional contributions. Adding $50/month to that from age 13 can push the total past $300,000. Time is the main ingredient.

What accounts earn compound interest for kids?

A few good options depending on your kid's age and whether they have earned income:

  • High-yield savings accounts (Ally, Marcus) — great starting point, currently 4%+ APY
  • Custodial brokerage accounts (Fidelity, Vanguard) — for investing in index funds
  • Custodial Roth IRA — best long-term option if your kid has earned income; contributions grow tax-free
  • 529 plans — specifically for education savings, but still compound growth

Standard savings accounts at big banks (Chase, Bank of America) typically pay 0.01% APY — that's not meaningfully compounding anything. Avoid those for this purpose.
