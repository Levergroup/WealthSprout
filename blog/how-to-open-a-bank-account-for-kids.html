The Day My Daughter Asked Where Money Lives

My 7-year-old came home with $20 from her grandma's birthday card, held it out to me, and asked: "Can I put it in the bank?" I didn't have a good answer — because she didn't have an account yet.

I'll be honest: I'd been meaning to open one for two years. It felt complicated. Joint accounts, custodial accounts, Social Security numbers, minimum deposits — every time I started researching, I closed the tab and told myself I'd do it next weekend.

That birthday cash was my wake-up call. We opened her account the next morning, and it took less than 15 minutes online. Here's everything I wish I'd known before that two-year delay.

When we sat down to open my daughter's account together, I let her type in her own name on the application form. She talked about that for a week. Sometimes the ritual of doing the thing matters as much as the thing itself — don't underestimate how much "her account" will mean to your kid.

Why a Real Bank Account Changes Everything

A piggy bank teaches kids that money exists. A real bank account teaches them how money actually works — interest, deposits, balances, and the satisfying experience of watching a number go up over time.

There's also something psychological happening. When your kid has an account in their name, money stops being abstract. It's their $47.83, and they check it. They ask about it. They start thinking before they spend.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) has found that kids who develop savings habits early are significantly more likely to save consistently as adults. A bank account is one of the lowest-effort, highest-impact moves you can make for your kid's financial future right now.

Joint vs. Custodial: Which Type Do You Actually Need?

Here's the part that used to make my eyes glaze over. Let me make it simple.

A joint account is exactly what it sounds like — you and your kid both have ownership. You can both deposit and withdraw, and either of you can see the full account. This is what most families use for everyday savings and spending.

A custodial account (sometimes called a UTMA or UGMA account) is different. You're the legal manager, your kid is the beneficiary, and once they hit the age of majority in your state — usually 18 or 21 — the account becomes fully theirs, automatically. These are more common for investment accounts or larger gift money, not everyday banking.

For teaching your kid how to save and spend thoughtfully? A joint savings or checking account at your regular bank is almost always the right call. You can read more about the specific account options in our deep-dive on kids bank accounts and how to choose the right one.

What to Look for in a Kids Bank Account

Not all kids accounts are created equal, and some of them are quietly terrible. Here's what actually matters:

  • No monthly maintenance fees — if a bank charges $5/month to hold $50, that's not a savings account, it's a drain
  • No minimum balance requirement (or a very low one — $25 is fine, $500 is not)
  • FDIC or NCUA insured — any legitimate bank or credit union will have this; it means your kid's money is protected up to $250,000
  • A decent APY — even 3.5–4.5% on a kids savings account adds up fast and makes interest tangible and exciting
  • Online and mobile access — your kid should be able to log in and see their balance; that visibility is half the lesson
  • Parental controls (if they'll have a debit card) — spending limits and real-time notifications are gold for ages 9+

Credit unions often beat big banks on all of these. Alliant Credit Union, for example, offers a kids savings account with no fees, no minimums, and a competitive APY. Capital One Kids Savings is another solid pick — zero fees, no minimum, and it links easily to a parent account.

How to Open a Bank Account for Kids: Step by Step

Here's the actual process, whether you're doing it in a branch or online (most people do it online now — it's faster).

Step 1: Choose your bank or credit union. Pick based on the criteria above. If you already have a checking account somewhere you like, check if they offer a joint kids savings account — that's the path of least resistance, since your accounts link automatically.

Step 2: Gather your documents. You'll need:

  • Your government-issued photo ID (driver's license or passport)
  • Your child's Social Security Number — yes, they need one; if your kid doesn't have one yet, you'll need to apply through the Social Security Administration first
  • Your child's birth certificate or passport for identity verification
  • A small opening deposit — anywhere from $0 to $25 depending on the bank

Step 3: Fill out the application. Online applications take about 10–15 minutes. You'll enter your info as the primary account holder and your child's info as the joint holder or minor. Some banks will ask you to upload a photo of your ID; have it ready.

Step 4: Fund the account. Transfer money from your existing checking account, or deposit cash at a branch. Even $20 is enough to get started — the habit matters more than the amount right now.

Step 5: Set up online access for your kid. Let them log in and see their balance. This is not optional. The visual of watching their savings grow is the whole point.

Opening an Account for Ages 5–8

For this age group, pure savings is the goal — no debit card yet, no spending account. You want a joint savings account with a high APY and zero fees.

The magic move here is making interest visible. When my daughter's account earned $0.47 in interest one month, she lost her mind — in the best way. "The bank paid me? For nothing?" Yes. That's the whole idea. You can build on that moment for years.

Opening an Account for Ages 9–13

At this age, a debit card becomes a real teaching tool — not a spending free-for-all, a tool. Your kid is ready to practice real spending decisions with real money, in real time, with you watching.

Look for a joint teen checking account (many banks offer these specifically for ages 8–17) or a debit card account like Greenlight, which comes with built-in parental spending controls. Greenlight lets you assign spending to specific categories — "restaurants: $15/week" — which forces actual decision-making, not just swiping.

The goal isn't protection from all mistakes. A $7 impulse buy they regret is worth more than a lecture from you about impulse buying. Check out our full breakdown of the best bank accounts for kids by age for more specific picks at this stage.

Frequently Asked Questions

What age can a child open a bank account?

Most banks allow kids to be added to a joint or custodial account at any age — even as a newborn. The child can't be the sole account holder until they're 18, but a parent or guardian co-signs and manages the account until then. Some banks, like Capital One Kids Savings, market specifically to kids as young as 0.

What documents do I need to open a kids bank account?

You'll typically need your government-issued ID, your child's Social Security Number, their birth certificate or passport for identity verification, and a small opening deposit — sometimes as low as $0 or $25. Requirements vary by bank, so check the specific institution's website before you go in.

What's the difference between a joint account and a custodial account for kids?

A joint account means both you and your child are co-owners with equal legal rights to the money. A custodial account (UTMA or UGMA) is managed by you as the custodian until your child reaches the age of majority — typically 18 or 21 depending on your state — at which point full control transfers to them automatically. For everyday savings and spending practice, most parents use a joint account.

Can I open a kids bank account online?

Yes — many banks and credit unions let you open a joint kids savings account entirely online in about 10 minutes. Ally Bank, Capital One, and many local credit unions all offer online applications. You'll upload ID documents digitally and fund the account with a transfer from your existing bank.

Are kids bank accounts FDIC insured?

Yes. Any account at an FDIC-insured bank — including kids savings and checking accounts — is protected up to $250,000 per depositor, per institution. Credit unions have equivalent protection through the NCUA. Your kid's money is just as safe as yours.

What's the best bank account for kids?

It depends on your goals. For younger kids (ages 5–8) focused on savings habits, look for no monthly fees, no minimum balance, and a competitive APY — Capital One Kids Savings and Alliant Credit Union are both strong picks. For older kids (ages 9–13) who need a debit card and spending practice, consider Greenlight or a joint teen checking account at your existing bank.
