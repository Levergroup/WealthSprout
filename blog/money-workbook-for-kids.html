What School Doesn't Teach Kids About Money (And Why That's a Problem)

Your kid can solve for X in an algebra equation but can't explain what a savings account actually does. That's not a parenting failure — that's a curriculum gap. Across the country, only 1 in 5 high schoolers are required to take a personal finance course, which means the vast majority of kids are hitting adulthood with zero structured money education.

The cost of that gap shows up fast. It looks like a college freshman racking up credit card debt before Thanksgiving. It looks like a 24-year-old who's never heard the word "compound interest" until they're already drowning in it.

Schools aren't designed to fix this — and that's not changing anytime soon. The responsibility lands on parents, and the good news is that you don't need a finance degree to do something about it. You just need a structure that does the heavy lifting for you.

Why a Money Workbook for Kids Works Better Than Lectures or Apps

Telling your kid "saving is important" is about as effective as telling them to eat their vegetables — they hear it, they nod, and nothing changes. A money workbook for kids works differently because it replaces passive listening with active doing.

Apps can be useful, but they often gamify money in ways that feel disconnected from real life. A physical workbook puts a pencil in your child's hand and asks them to make actual decisions — on paper, with real numbers that reflect their real world.

The act of writing down a savings goal, tracking spending across two weeks, and seeing their progress in their own handwriting builds a kind of ownership that no screen can replicate. It's tactile. It's personal. And it sticks.

How to Use This Workbook at Home Without Being a Finance Expert

You don't need to know what a Roth IRA is to use this workbook effectively. Seriously. The workbook is written for kids, which means the explanations are clear enough for a parent who hasn't thought about compound interest since high school.

The simplest approach: set aside 20 minutes once a week. Pick one section, sit with your child, and work through it together. You're not teaching — you're facilitating. The workbook does the instructing. You provide the conversation.

A few tips that make the experience stick:

  • Connect workbook exercises to real money your child has — allowance, gift money, or earnings from small jobs around the house
  • Let your child make the pencil marks and decisions without correcting them mid-exercise — mistakes made on paper are the cheapest lessons they'll ever get
  • Talk about your own money decisions when they come up naturally, using the workbook's language as a shared reference point

Frequently Asked Questions

What age is a money workbook for kids most effective?

Most money workbooks for kids work well starting around age 5 or 6, when children can handle basic counting and simple concepts like saving and spending. That said, you know your child best — some kids are ready a little earlier, and many teens still get a ton of value from structured money activities too.

How do I get my child excited about learning financial skills?

Tie money lessons to things your child already cares about — like saving up for a toy, a game, or a fun outing. When kids can see a real goal at the end of the page, working through activities feels less like homework and a lot more like a plan they're actually in charge of.

What topics should a good money workbook for kids cover?

Look for a workbook that goes beyond just counting coins — it should cover earning, saving, spending wisely, and giving. The best ones also sneak in goal-setting and basic budgeting, so kids leave with habits they'll actually carry into adulthood.

Can I use a kids' money workbook without giving my child an allowance?

Absolutely — you don't need an allowance in place for the lessons to land. Many of the activities use everyday moments, like grocery shopping or birthday money, to make the concepts feel real and relevant to your child's life right now.

How often should my child work through money activities each week?

Even 10 to 15 minutes once or twice a week is enough to build real momentum over time. Consistency matters more than cramming, so pick a low-pressure time — like after school or weekend mornings — and let it become a regular part of your routine.
