When My Daughter Got Her First Job (and I Realized What I'd Missed)

My 16-year-old just started her first real job at a local coffee shop. $13.50 an hour, three shifts a week. She came home with her first paycheck—$287.82 after taxes—so proud she took a photo of it.

And then she asked me something that stopped me cold: "Mom, should I be doing something with this money to build credit? My friend's dad said she's already behind."

Here's the thing: I didn't even think about credit until I was 22 and got denied for my first apartment because I had no credit history. Zero. I had money in savings. I had a job. But landlords don't care about that—they care about your credit score.

I wish someone had told me at 16 that your first job isn't just about making money. It's about proving to the financial system that you exist and you're responsible. Because here's what nobody tells you: building credit takes time. You can't rush it. The earlier you start, the better off you'll be when you actually need it at 19, 20, 21.

So when my daughter asked that question, I didn't dismiss it. We sat down that night and mapped out exactly how she could use her first job to start building credit. And that's exactly what I'm going to show you how to do.

Why Your First Job Is Actually Your First Credit Opportunity

Let's talk about what credit actually is, because I didn't understand this until I was 34 and paying off $31K in debt.

Credit is proof that you can borrow money and pay it back on time. That's it. It's not about how much money you make or how much you have in savings. It's a track record.

According to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, your credit score is calculated based on five main factors:

  • Payment history (35%): Do you pay on time?
  • Credit utilization (30%): How much of your available credit are you using?
  • Length of credit history (15%): How long have you had credit?
  • Credit mix (10%): Do you have different types of credit?
  • New credit (10%): Have you opened a bunch of accounts recently?

Notice that third one? Length of credit history. That's why your first job matters. The sooner you start, the longer your history will be when you actually need credit for an apartment, a car, or a phone plan.

Here's what having good credit will do for you by the time you're 20:

  • Get approved for apartment rentals (most landlords check credit)
  • Qualify for car loans with lower interest rates (we're talking thousands of dollars in savings)
  • Get better rates on car insurance (yes, really—insurance companies check credit)
  • Get approved for phone plans without a co-signer
  • Eventually qualify for mortgages, business loans, and better credit cards

Your first job gives you something crucial: income. And income is what allows you to get credit in the first place. Banks want to see that you can pay back what you borrow. A job proves that.

Your First Paycheck: What to Do First

You just got your first paycheck. It's sitting in your bank account (or you're holding a paper check, wondering what to do with it). Here's exactly what to do, in order:

Step 1: Open a checking account if you don't have one

If you're under 18, you'll need a parent to co-sign on a teen checking account. Chase, Bank of America, and most credit unions offer these with no monthly fees. Deposit that check.

Step 2: Set aside 20% for future you

Open a high-yield savings account (Ally, Marcus, or Discover all allow teen accounts with a parent co-signer). Put 20% of every paycheck there. If you made $287 like my daughter, that's $57.40.

Why? Because in 6-12 months, you're going to use some of this money to open a secured credit card (more on that below). The rest is your emergency fund.

Step 3: Keep 10% for fun money

Seriously. You worked for this. Take $28 and spend it on something you want. No guilt. Building good money habits means enjoying some of what you earn.

Step 4: Use the rest for expenses

This covers gas, food, clothes, activities—whatever you're responsible for paying. The remaining $201 is yours to spend on life.

For more details on exactly how to split up that first paycheck, check out our guide on what to do with your first paycheck as a teenager.

Why this matters for credit

Building credit requires two things: consistent income and a track record of responsible money management. By saving 20% of every paycheck, you're proving to yourself (and eventually to banks) that you can handle money responsibly.

How to Start Building Credit at 16, 17, or 18

Okay, here's the actionable part. How you build credit depends on how old you are right now.

If you're 16 or 17: Become an authorized user

You can't get your own credit card until you're 18, but you can become an authorized user on a parent's credit card right now.

Here's how it works:

  1. Ask a parent who has good credit (pays on time, keeps balances low) to add you as an authorized user on one of their credit cards
  2. They request an authorized user card in your name from their credit card company
  3. The card company reports that account to your credit file
  4. You start building credit history immediately, even if you never use the card

Important: The parent keeps control of the account. They can set spending limits on your card or just keep it in a drawer. The point isn't for you to spend—it's for you to inherit their positive payment history.

My daughter became an authorized user on my Chase Freedom card in October. By December, she had a credit score of 702. She never even touched the card.

This strategy only works if your parent:

  • Pays the bill on time every month
  • Keeps their balance below 30% of their credit limit
  • Has had the card for a while (older is better)

If your parent has bad credit or misses payments, this will hurt you. Choose carefully.

If you're 18: Open your own secured credit card

Once you turn 18 and have a job, you can open your own secured credit card. This is the fastest way to build credit in your own name.

A secured credit card works like this:

  1. You deposit money (usually $200-500) with the card company
  2. That deposit becomes your credit limit
  3. You use the card like a normal credit card
  4. You pay the bill every month
  5. After 6-12 months of on-time payments, most companies "graduate" you to a regular card and give your deposit back

Best secured cards for teens with first jobs:

  • Discover it® Secured: $200 minimum deposit, cash back on purchases, no annual fee
  • Capital One Platinum Secured: Sometimes approves you with deposits as low as $49-$200
  • Chime Credit Builder: No deposit required, but you need a Chime checking account with direct deposit

Here's exactly how to use it:

  1. Put one small recurring charge on it—like Spotify ($10.99/month) or Netflix
  2. Set up autopay to pay the full balance every month from your checking account
  3. Never carry a balance. Ever.
  4. Check your credit score monthly (Discover and Capital One give you free access)

Within 6 months, you'll have a real credit score. Within 12 months, you'll have a "good" score (670+).

If your parents won't help or don't have good credit

This was my situation. My parents had terrible credit and couldn't help me. Here's what to do:

  1. Wait until you're 18
  2. Save $200-500 from your job for a secured card deposit
  3. Open a secured card in your own name
  4. Follow the strategy above

It takes a little longer, but you'll still have a solid credit score by 19 or 20. That's years ahead of most people.

Common Credit Mistakes Teens Make (and How to Avoid Them)

I've seen teens with first jobs make the same credit mistakes over and over. Here are the big ones:

Mistake #1: Carrying a balance to "build credit faster"

This is a myth that costs people thousands of dollars. You do not need to carry a balance and pay interest to build credit.

Here's what actually happens: Your card company reports to credit bureaus once a month, usually on your statement date. As long as you use your card and pay it off before the due date, you're building credit.

What to do instead: Pay your full balance every month. Set up autopay so you never miss a payment. You'll build credit just as fast and pay $0 in interest.

Mistake #2: Maxing out your credit card

Your credit utilization—how much of your available credit you're using—makes up 30% of your credit score. Using more than 30% hurts your score.

If you have a $500 credit limit, don't spend more than $150 in a month. Ideally, keep it under $50.

What to do instead: Use your card for one small thing each month. Put it on autopay. Don't treat it like free money—it's a credit-building tool.

Mistake #3: Opening multiple cards at once

Every time you apply for credit, it creates a "hard inquiry" on your credit report. Too many inquiries in a short time tanks your score.

What to do instead: Start with one card. Use it responsibly for 12 months. Then, if you want another card, apply for one. Slow and steady wins.

Mistake #4: Ignoring your credit score

You can't improve what you don't track. Teens I work with are often surprised when I ask, "What's your credit score?" and they have no idea.

What to do instead: Sign up for free credit monitoring. If you have a Discover or Capital One card, they give you free FICO scores. You can also use Credit Karma (free) or sign up at AnnualCreditReport.com to see your full credit report once a year.

Mistake #5: Thinking you're too young to start

The biggest mistake is waiting. According to Next Gen Personal Finance, fewer than 10% of high school students have any credit history. That means if you start now, you're ahead of 90% of your peers.

What to do instead: Start today. Right now. If you're 16 or 17, ask your parents about becoming an authorized user. If you're 18, start saving for a secured card deposit.

Practical Application: What to Do This Week

Enough theory. Here's your action plan for the next 7 days:

Monday: Check if you can become an authorized user

Sit down with a parent and ask: "Do you have a credit card you've had for a while that you pay on time every month? Can you add me as an authorized user?"

If they say yes, ask them to call their credit card company this week. It takes 10 minutes.

If they say no or they don't have good credit, that's okay. Move to Plan B.

Tuesday: Open a savings account for your credit card deposit

If you're 18 or will be soon, start saving $200-300 for a secured card deposit. Open a separate savings account and label it "Credit Card Deposit."

Set up automatic transfers: every time you get paid, move $40-50 to this account. In 4-6 paychecks, you'll have enough to open a secured card.

Wednesday: Research secured credit cards

Spend 20 minutes comparing:

  • Discover it® Secured
  • Capital One Platinum Secured
  • Chime Credit Builder

Read reviews on Investopedia and check each card's approval requirements.

Thursday: Set up a simple budget

Download a free app like Mint or YNAB (You Need a Budget). Connect your checking account. Categorize your spending for the past month.

Why? Because building credit requires consistent income and responsible spending. You need to prove to yourself that you can handle a credit card before you get one.

Friday: Check your credit report (if you're 18+)

Go to AnnualCreditReport.com and request your free credit report. See if anything is already on file.

If you became an authorized user, your parent's card might already be showing up. If you have no file at all, that's normal—you're starting from scratch.

Saturday: Set up autopay for one bill

Practice responsible payment habits now. Pick one recurring expense—your phone bill, Spotify, car insurance—and set it up on autopay from your checking account.

This builds the habit of never missing a payment. When you get a credit card, you'll already have this muscle memory.

Sunday: Talk to one person who's done this

Find someone in their early 20s who has good credit. Ask them: "How did you start building credit? What do you wish you'd known?"

Real-world advice from someone just a few years ahead of you is gold.

What People Are Actually Wondering

These are real questions from teens with first jobs who are trying to figure out credit:

"I'm 17 and just got my first job. Should I get a credit card to start building credit? My parents say wait until college but I feel like I'm already behind."

— Reddit user on r/personalfinance

My answer: You're not behind, but you're right to think about this now. At 17, you can't get your own card, but you can become an authorized user on a parent's card. That's actually better than waiting until college because you'll have 1-2 years of credit history by the time you turn 18.

Your parents might be worried you'll overspend. Show them this article. Explain that you don't even need to use the card—just being an authorized user builds your credit.

"What's the fastest way for a teen with a first job to build credit? I make $400/month and want to start now."

— Quora user

My answer: Fastest way at 16-17: become an authorized user. You'll have a credit score within 1-2 months.

Fastest way at 18+: open a secured credit card with a $200-300 deposit, put one small recurring charge on it (like Spotify), set up autopay, and pay it off in full every month. You'll have a real credit score in 3-6 months.

Your $400/month income is perfect for this. Save $200 for the deposit, then use the card for $20-30 of regular spending each month.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you build credit at 16 or 17 with a first job?

Yes, but not by opening your own credit card. You can build credit at 16 or 17 by becoming an authorized user on a parent's credit card. As long as the parent pays on time and keeps balances low, their positive payment history will appear on your credit report. This is actually the fastest way to build credit as a teen because you inherit their credit history length. You don't need to use the card or even have it in your possession—just being listed as an authorized user builds your credit.

How much of my first job paycheck should I save to build credit?

Save at least $200-300 from your first few paychecks if you're planning to open a secured credit card at 18. Most secured cards require a minimum deposit of $200. If you're making $300-400 per paycheck, try to save $50-75 per paycheck for 4-6 paychecks. This gives you the deposit money plus a small emergency fund. The secured card deposit isn't "spent"—you'll get it back after 6-12 months of on-time payments when the card company upgrades you to a regular card. Think of it as money you're temporarily setting aside, not money you're losing.

What credit card should I get with my first job at 18?

If you're 18 with a first job and no credit history, start with a secured credit card. The best options are Discover it® Secured (requires $200 deposit, offers cash back, no annual fee), Capital One Platinum Secured (sometimes approves with deposits as low as $49), or Chime Credit Builder (no deposit required but you need a Chime checking account with direct deposit from your job). Avoid cards with annual fees or high interest rates. Your goal isn't to spend—it's to build credit. Put one small recurring charge on the card (like Spotify for $11/month), set up autopay to pay it off in full, and within 6-12 months you'll have a good credit score.

Will getting a credit card with my first job hurt my credit score?

Opening your first credit card will cause a small, temporary dip in your credit score (usually 5-10 points) because of the hard inquiry and because you're opening a new account. But this recovers within a few months, and the long-term benefit far outweighs the short-term dip. As long as you pay on time and keep your balance low (under 30% of your limit), your score will steadily increase over the next 6-12 months. What will hurt your score: missing payments, maxing out your card, or applying for multiple cards at once. If you use your card responsibly, it will only help your credit, not hurt it.

How long does it take to build credit with a first job?

If you become an authorized user on a parent's card, you can have a credit score within 1-2 months. If you open your own secured credit card at 18, it takes 3-6 months to generate a credit score, and 12-18 months to build a "good" score (670+). The key is consistent, on-time payments. Your credit score is based on your payment history over time, so there's no way to rush it. But starting at 16-18 means you'll have 1-3 years of credit history by the time you're 20, which puts you way ahead of most people your age. The earlier you start, the better off you'll be when you need credit for an apartment, car, or phone plan.

Can I build credit without a credit card if I have a first job?

Yes, but it's slower and harder. Some alternatives: Experian Boost lets you add utility and phone bill payments to your credit report (but not all lenders check this). Self or Kikoff offer credit-builder loans where you make monthly payments and they report to credit bureaus. Some landlords report rent payments to credit bureaus if you ask. However, these methods are slower and less effective than a credit card. Credit cards are the fastest, most reliable way to build credit as a teen because they're specifically designed to report to all three credit bureaus every month. If you're nervous about credit cards, start with a secured card with a low limit ($200-300) so you can't overspend.
