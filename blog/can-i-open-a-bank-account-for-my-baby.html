Can I Open a Bank Account for My Baby?

Is it too early to open a bank account for a newborn?

Short answer: No. You can open a bank account for your baby the day they're born — technically the day you get their Social Security number, which usually takes a week or two.

I didn't know this with my first kid. I thought you had to wait until they were old enough to, I don't know, sign their name? Understand what money is? I opened my daughter's first savings account when she was 4, and even then I felt like I was ahead of the curve.

Then I did the math on what I'd missed. If I'd put $50/month into a savings account earning 4% interest starting at birth instead of age 4, she'd have had an extra $3,200 by the time she turned 18. That's just from starting four years earlier. That's the thing about compound interest — time matters more than the amount.

So yes, you can open a bank account for your baby. And if you're thinking about it, you're already ahead of where I was.

Why opening a bank account for your baby actually matters

Here's what I wish someone had told me: Opening a bank account for your baby isn't about teaching them to balance a checkbook at 6 months old. It's about giving them a financial head start before they even know what money is.

Three reasons this matters:

1. Compound interest works best with time. If you put $100/month into a savings account earning 4% APY starting when your kid is born, by the time they're 18 they'll have about $27,000. If you wait until they're 10, same contributions, same rate? About $11,500. You gave up $15,500 by waiting. Time is the most underrated ingredient.

2. It's a place for gifts to actually grow. My daughter got $600 in cash for her first birthday. We spent maybe $100 on diapers and I stuck the rest in a savings account. Seven years later, with interest and additional birthday deposits, that account has over $4,000. If I'd left it in a drawer or spent it on baby gear she outgrew in three months, it'd be gone.

3. You're building infrastructure for later. When your kid is 7 and wants to save up for a toy, or 14 and gets their first paycheck from babysitting, the account is already there. You're not scrambling to set it up. You've already created the habit of money goes into this account. That's half the battle.

I didn't understand this with my first kid. With my second, I opened a savings account the week we got his Social Security number. I put $25/month in automatically. He's 6 now, has no idea the account exists, and there's $2,100 sitting there earning interest. When he's old enough to grasp what that means, I'll hand him the login and say, "This is your head start."

Common mistakes parents make (I made two of these)

Mistake #1: Waiting until your kid is "old enough to understand." I did this with my daughter. I thought it wouldn't mean anything to her if she couldn't grasp the concept. But the account isn't for her to understand at age 2 — it's for the money to grow while she's 2. You can teach her about it later. The clock on compound interest starts now.

Mistake #2: Choosing an account with fees. Some parents open a custodial savings account at their local bank because it's convenient, then realize there's a $5/month maintenance fee unless they keep a $500 minimum balance. Over 18 years, that's $1,080 in fees. For a baby. Use a no-fee account. They exist, and they're easy to find (I'll list them below).

Mistake #3: Thinking a piggy bank is enough. Piggy banks are great for teaching kids to see and count money. But they don't earn interest, they're not FDIC-insured, and let's be honest — when you need $20 for the babysitter, that piggy bank is tempting. A bank account keeps the money separate, growing, and protected. Do both: piggy bank for hands-on learning, bank account for actual growth.

Mistake #4: Not knowing the difference between account types. I opened a joint savings account for my daughter, which meant I was a co-owner. That's fine, but it also means the IRS counts it as my asset, not hers. A custodial account (UTMA or UGMA) legally belongs to the child, with you as the custodian. This distinction matters for taxes and financial aid down the line. I didn't learn this until I was 34 and refinancing our mortgage. Wish I'd known at 28.

Mistake #5: Only putting in birthday money and nothing else. Grandma's $50 check is great. But if that's the only time you add to the account, it's not going to grow much. Even $10 or $25/month on autopilot makes a massive difference over 18 years. Consistency beats big one-time deposits.

What actually works: Specific accounts and steps

Here's what I recommend, broken down by account type and bank.

Best savings accounts for babies (ages 0-2)

Ally Bank Online Savings Account: No minimum balance, no monthly fees, currently offers around 4.00% APY (rates change, check their site). You open a custodial savings account in your baby's name with you as the custodian. You'll need your baby's Social Security number, birth certificate, and your ID. I use Ally for my son's account. It's easy, the app is clear, and I set up automatic $25 transfers every month.

Capital One Kids Savings Account: No fees, no minimums, and they've designed it with kids in mind — though for a baby, you're the only one logging in. Interest rates are typically around 3.50-4.00% APY. You can open it online in about 10 minutes. My friend uses this for her twins and loves that it links to her own Capital One checking for easy transfers.

Marcus by Goldman Sachs: High-yield savings with no fees and no minimum deposit. Rates hover around 4.00-4.30% APY. You can open a custodial account for your baby. The downside: no physical branches, so everything is online. For a baby account, that's usually fine.

Local credit unions: If you're already a credit union member, check if they offer custodial savings accounts for minors. Many do, often with competitive rates and lower fees than big banks. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau has resources on comparing account options.

If you want to invest (not just save)

A savings account is great for cash that's safe and liquid. But if you want to invest for long-term growth — say, for college or a down payment on their first house — consider a custodial brokerage account (UTMA/UGMA).

Fidelity Youth Account: Technically for teens 13-17, but Fidelity also offers custodial brokerage accounts you can open for a baby. No account fees, no minimums. You can invest in stocks, ETFs, mutual funds. I have a small UTMA for my daughter at Fidelity where I put $50/month into VTI (Vanguard Total Stock Market ETF). Over 10 years, assuming 8% average annual returns, that'll be about $9,000. Way more than a savings account.

Charles Schwab Custodial Account: Another solid option. No account minimums, no trading fees for stocks and ETFs. You can set up automatic investments. I know parents who put $100/month into SCHB (Schwab's broad market ETF) for their kids.

Vanguard UTMA/UGMA: If you're into low-cost index funds (I am), Vanguard is the classic choice. You can open a custodial account and invest in funds like VTSAX or VTI. Minimum initial investment is usually $1,000 for mutual funds, but $0 for ETFs if you buy through their brokerage.

Important note: Money in a UTMA/UGMA is legally your child's. When they turn 18 (or 21, depending on your state), they get full control. You can't take it back or dictate how they use it. This is why I do both: a savings account I control, and a smaller UTMA I'm teaching them to manage as they grow.

Documents you'll need

Every bank asks for roughly the same things:

  • Your baby's Social Security number
  • Your baby's birth certificate (certified copy or original)
  • Your government-issued ID (driver's license or passport)
  • Proof of address (utility bill, lease, bank statement)
  • Sometimes a second form of ID for the child (insurance card, hospital record)

If you're opening the account online, you'll upload photos or scans. If you're doing it in person, bring the physical documents. Most online banks make this very easy — I opened my son's Ally account while he napped, took me 15 minutes.

Step-by-step: Opening a savings account for your baby

  1. Get your baby's Social Security number. You usually apply at the hospital when your baby is born. It arrives in the mail in 1-3 weeks. You need this before you can open any financial account.
  2. Choose a bank. I recommend starting with a high-yield savings account like Ally or Capital One Kids for simplicity and safety. Compare APY (interest rate), fees, and minimums.
  3. Decide on account type. Custodial savings account means the money is legally your child's, you're the custodian until they're 18-21. Joint account means you're both owners. Custodial is more common and better for financial aid purposes later.
  4. Gather documents. SSN, birth certificate, your ID, proof of address.
  5. Open the account online or in person. Most banks let you do this in under 20 minutes online. You'll answer some questions, upload documents, and link a funding source (your checking account).
  6. Make your first deposit. Even $25 or $50 is fine. The goal is to open the account and start the clock.
  7. Set up automatic transfers. This is the step that matters. $10, $25, $50, whatever fits your budget. Automatic monthly transfers turn this from "I'll add money when I remember" into "this account grows every month without me thinking about it."

One thing I didn't expect: how satisfying it feels to watch the balance grow. My son's account started with $50. Six years later, it's over $2,100. He has no idea. But I know that when he's 16 and wants to buy a used car, or 20 and needs a security deposit on an apartment, this is sitting there. That's a gift.

Age-by-age breakdown: What kind of account when

Not every account makes sense at every age. Here's how I think about it:

Ages 0-2 (babies/infants)

Best account type: Custodial savings account at a high-yield online bank (Ally, Capital One, Marcus).

Why: Your baby isn't interacting with money yet. The goal is to park cash somewhere safe where it earns interest. A savings account is FDIC-insured (up to $250,000), easy to manage, and you can add to it regularly.

What to do: Open the account as soon as you have their SSN. Set up a small automatic monthly transfer ($10-$50, whatever fits). Add birthday/holiday money from relatives. Don't touch it.

Also consider: If you're thinking long-term (10+ years), open a custodial brokerage account (UTMA/UGMA) and put $25-$100/month into a low-cost index fund like VTI or VOO. Let it ride. By the time they're 18, this could be $30,000-$60,000 depending on contributions and market returns.

Ages 3-5 (toddlers/preschool)

Best account type: Keep the custodial savings account running in the background. Introduce a piggy bank or 3-jar system (spend, save, give) for hands-on learning.

Why: At this age, kids can start to understand "money buys things" and "saving means waiting." But they're not ready for a debit card or app. A piggy bank lets them see and touch money. The savings account keeps growing in the background, earning interest.

What to do: Give them a small allowance or let them earn money for simple chores. Let them divide it into jars. When the "save" jar gets to $20 or $50, take them to the bank (or show them on your phone) and "deposit" it into their savings account. This is when you start connecting the dots between the physical money and the account.

Ages 6-12 (elementary/middle school)

Best account type: Keep the custodial savings account. Consider adding a debit card designed for kids (Greenlight, GoHenry, BusyKid) if they're ready to manage small purchases.

Why: By age 6-7, most kids can grasp that the numbers in an app represent real money. They're also starting to want to buy things independently (snacks, toys, games). A debit card with parental controls lets them practice spending without cash. The savings account is for long-term goals.

What to do: Show them their savings account balance. Explain interest: "The bank pays you a little bit of money every month because your money is helping them." Set a savings goal together (a toy, a trip, a bike). Use the debit card for short-term spending. Keep contributing to the savings account.

For specific tips on helping older kids earn their own money, we've got a whole guide on that.

Ages 13-17 (teens)

Best account type: A teen checking account (Fidelity Youth, Capital One MONEY, Chase High School Checking) with a debit card. Keep the custodial savings account for long-term goals.

Why: Teens need practice managing money in real-world scenarios — buying lunch, splitting costs with friends, budgeting for clothes. A checking account with a debit card gives them independence with guardrails (you can still monitor and set limits).

What to do: Let them manage their checking account. Encourage them to keep a portion of any earnings or gifts in their savings account. If they have a job, help them open a Roth IRA (they can contribute up to $7,000/year or their earned income, whichever is less). This is when the money lessons you started at age 0 start paying off.

Age 18+ (young adults)

At 18 (or 21 in some states), the custodial account legally transfers to them. You no longer have control. They can withdraw it, invest it, or spend it. This is why teaching financial literacy along the way matters so much.

If they've been involved in managing the account since age 6 or 7, this transition is smoother. If you've been hiding it from them and surprise them with $20,000 on their 18th birthday, results may vary.

What parents are actually asking

What documents do I need to open a bank account for my baby?

You'll need your baby's Social Security number, their birth certificate (original or certified copy), your government-issued ID (driver's license or passport), and proof of your address (utility bill or bank statement). Some banks also ask for a second form of ID for the child. If you're opening online, you'll upload photos or scans of these documents.

Can I open a savings account in my baby's name only?

Not exactly. Minors can't legally own accounts on their own until they're 18. You'll open either a custodial account (you manage it until they're 18-21, depending on your state) or a joint account (you're both on it). Custodial accounts are more common for babies because the money legally belongs to your child — you're just the steward.

What's the difference between a savings account and a UTMA/UGMA account?

A savings account at a bank holds cash and earns interest — it's FDIC-insured and safe. A UTMA/UGMA account (Uniform Transfers/Gifts to Minors Act) is a custodial brokerage account where you can invest in stocks, bonds, ETFs, and mutual funds. UTMA/UGMA accounts have higher growth potential but also market risk. Many parents do both: a savings account for short-term goals and cash reserves, and a UTMA for long-term investing.

Will my baby's savings account affect financial aid?

Yes, but it depends on the account type. Money in a custodial account (savings or UTMA/UGMA) is considered the child's asset. The FAFSA formula counts 20% of a child's assets vs. 5.64% of a parent's assets. So if your child has $10,000 in a custodial account, $2,000 is expected to go toward college costs. If that same $10,000 is in a 529 plan owned by a parent, only $564 counts. This matters more as your kid approaches college age — for a baby, you have 18 years to strategize.

Can grandparents open a bank account for my baby?

Yes, in most cases. Grandparents can open a custodial savings account with themselves as the custodian (not the parent). They'll need the child's Social Security number, birth certificate, and their own ID. Some banks require a parent's signature or notification. Alternatively, grandparents can open an account in their own name and designate the baby as a beneficiary, then transfer it later.

What happens to the account when my child turns 18?

With a custodial account, your child gains full legal control at the age of majority — 18 in most states, 21 in others. At that point, the account transfers entirely to them. They can withdraw it, invest it, or blow it on concert tickets. You no longer have any legal say. That's why teaching financial skills early matters — by the time they're 18, they should know how to manage what you've built for them.

Next steps

If you're reading this and your baby is napping in the next room, here's what I'd do this week:

  1. Make sure you have your baby's Social Security number. If you don't have it yet, that's step one.
  2. Pick a high-yield savings account. Ally and Capital One Kids are both great. Compare rates, but honestly, any account with no fees and 4%+ APY is solid.
  3. Open the account. Gather your documents, go to the bank's website, and just do it. It takes 15-20 minutes. You can do it while your kid is asleep or while you're waiting for your coffee to brew.
  4. Make your first deposit. Even $25. Just to get it started.
  5. Set up a small automatic transfer. $10, $25, $50 a month. Whatever fits your budget. Automate it so you don't have to remember.

That's it. You've just given your kid a financial head start.
