Weekly Chore Chart Template for Kids: Set Up One That Actually Lasts

I printed a gorgeous, color-coded chore chart three years ago and stuck it on the fridge. I felt like a genius. By Thursday my daughter had "lost" the marker, my son had drawn a dinosaur over Wednesday, and I was the only person in the house who remembered the chart existed.

If that sounds familiar, you're in good company. I taught middle school math for years, and I still got this wrong. The chart wasn't the problem. The design was. In this post I'll walk you through a weekly chore chart template you can build in about 30 minutes, and why each piece of it exists.

The Problem: Why Most Chore Charts Fail in the First Week

Here's what I've seen go wrong, in my house and in the messages parents send me.

The chart is a wall of tasks. My first version had 14 items per kid per day. A 6-year-old looks at that and sees a mountain, not a checklist. Honestly, so did I.

Everything is mixed together. Making your bed and washing the car sat in the same column with the same stickers. My kids had no idea which jobs were "just what we do" and which ones earned money. So they negotiated everything, every day.

There's no rhythm. "Clean your room" floats around with no anchor. Kids (and tired parents) do better when a task lives at a specific time, like before breakfast or right after school.

It's not built to survive. Paper charts tear, markers vanish, and by week two there's a crumpled sheet on the floor.

The parent is the enforcement. If the chart only works when you're nagging, it's not a system. It's a second job for you.

Our first chart lasted four days. The one we use now has survived two school years, three marker brands, and one very enthusiastic juice spill. The difference wasn't discipline. It was making the chart boring, predictable, and wipeable. — Maya

The Stats: What Research Actually Says About Kids and Chores

I went looking for one definitive study that says "do X and your kid will thrive," and the honest answer is that the evidence is thinner than the parenting internet makes it sound. So here's what I can tell you, and what's just parent wisdom.

What's common practice:

- The "half-your-age" rule. A popular allowance guideline is half your kid's age in dollars per week. A 10-year-old gets $5. A 6-year-old gets $3. It's not a law of nature, just a quick starting point that keeps you from overthinking it.

- The national average. You'll often see an average allowance figure of about $13.15 per week. I'd treat that carefully. My guess (not a finding) is that older kids and bigger budgets pull it up. The half-your-age number is a better fit for the 5-to-10 crowd.

- Visual charts with time blocks. Charts split into morning, after-school, and evening are the format parents report working best. That matches what I saw in my classroom. Kids follow a routine they can *see* far better than one they have to remember.

What parents describe consistently:

- Kids can start contributing as toddlers. Think putting toys in a bin or carrying their plate to the counter.

- The word "chore" carries baggage. Some families say "responsibilities" instead, and it changes how kids see their role.

- Separating expected daily tasks from optional paid work cuts down on arguments.

For the money side of this, Next Gen Personal Finance and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau both publish free youth financial education resources worth bookmarking.

I'm telling you where the evidence ends because I'd rather you build something that works for your kid than follow a number I can't back up.

The Solution: What a Weekly Chore Chart Actually Needs

After a lot of trial and error, my chart comes down to five pieces.

1. Two categories, clearly separated.

- *Daily responsibilities* are unpaid: making the bed, clearing your plate, putting dirty clothes in the hamper. These are part of living in the family.

- *Extra jobs* are paid: washing the car, raking leaves, mowing for older kids. These are optional, and your kid picks them.

2. Three time blocks. Morning, after-school, and evening. Every task gets a home. "Make bed" lives in the morning block, so nobody has to wonder when it happens.

3. Three to five tasks per kid, max. Fewer tasks, done consistently, beat a long list done once.

4. Seven day columns. Monday through Sunday, with a box to check for each task. Kids love seeing a row fill up.

5. A surface that resets. Laminate the chart or slide it into a plastic sheet protector, then use dry-erase markers. Wipe it every Sunday night and start fresh. One chart lasts the whole year.

If you'd rather not build the grid from scratch, I made a ready-to-print version in our kids chore chart printable post.

The Step-by-Step Setup: From Blank Page to Working System

Grab a sheet of paper and a pen. This takes about 30 minutes.

Step 1: Make two lists

Draw a line down the middle. On the left, write Daily Responsibilities (unpaid). On the right, write Extra Jobs (paid).

Age-appropriate daily examples:

- Ages 4-5: put pajamas in the hamper, feed the pet with a scoop you've pre-measured, put toys in a bin

- Ages 6-8: make bed, clear plate, pack backpack, set the table

- Ages 9-10: all of the above, plus unload the dishwasher and tidy the bathroom sink

Paid extras with sample prices: wash the car ($3-5), pull weeds for 15 minutes ($2), organize the garage shelf ($4), mow the lawn for older kids ($8-10). Set prices based on your family's budget. These are what worked for ours.

Step 2: Pick 3-5 daily tasks per kid

Resist the urge to cover everything. I started my 6-year-old with three: make bed, clear plate, backpack by the door. We added a fourth after a month.

Step 3: Build the grid

Rows are your three time blocks. Columns are Monday through Sunday.

- Morning: make bed, get dressed, pajamas in hamper

- After-school: unpack backpack, snack dishes to the sink

- Evening: clear dinner plate, 5-minute toy tidy, set out tomorrow's clothes

Put the paid extras in a separate "Job Board" box at the bottom of the page.

Step 4: Decide on allowance before you launch

If you're using allowance, use the half-your-age rule as a starting point. A 6-year-old gets $3 a week, a 10-year-old gets $5. For more on how this works in practice, Investopedia has a plain-English breakdown of allowance models. Tell your kid what the number is before the first week starts, so nobody feels surprised later.

Step 5: Make it reusable

Slide the chart into a plastic sheet protector or laminate it. Tape a dry-erase marker to a string beside it. This one detail saved me more nagging than anything else, because "I can't find a marker" stopped being an excuse.

Step 6: Hold a 10-minute kickoff

Sit down together on a Sunday. Let your kid choose the color for their column and decide where the chart hangs. Walk through one full day out loud: "Morning block, you make your bed. Then what?" Then stop talking and let them do it.

Step 7: Do a Sunday reset

Every Sunday, wipe the chart, count the checks together, and pay out allowance or job money. Ten minutes. Skipping this is the most common reason charts die in week three. (I've skipped it. I know.)

What School Doesn't Teach About Responsibility and Money

I taught math for years, and I can tell you that no class ever covered this: money and effort are connected, and the connection is learned at home.

Kids can solve for x and still have no idea why a paycheck exists. A chore chart is a surprisingly good bridge. When your kid sees that clearing plates is simply what family members do, while washing the car earns $4, they're learning the difference between *contribution* and *income*. That's a concept many adults still fumble.

It also opens the door to everything else. Once your kid earns a few dollars, they need somewhere to put it. We use a 3-jar system in our house: spend, save, give. Suddenly the chart isn't just about a clean kitchen. It's the first step in a real money habit. And when your kid gets older and the stakes get bigger, our guide on money management for teens picks up where this leaves off.

I'll admit I didn't connect those dots until my daughter asked, at age 7, "Why do grown-ups get money for going to work but I just get a sticker?" Fair point, kid.

What Parents Are Actually Asking

When I read through parent discussions online, two themes come up again and again: what to *call* the work, and how to separate expected tasks from paid ones.

On the language question, one parent wrote:

"I don't call them chores. We call them 'responsibilities' so they know it's part of being in the family, not a job."

[Reddit parent, via Scary Mommy](https://www.scarymommy.com/parenting/do-kids-still-do-chores-reddit-parent-debate)

I love this. I switched our wording a year ago, and the eye-rolling dropped noticeably. It's a small change, but "responsibility" sounds like something you *have*, while "chore" sounds like something being done *to* you.

On the paid vs. unpaid question, another parent shared:

"We do unpaid daily tasks (bed, dishes) and paid extra chores (yard work, car wash). The kids understand the difference."

[Reddit parent](https://reddit.sentinel-team.org/posts/1skb7yo/snapshots/2026-04-17T23%3A57%3A04.604915Z)

That's the exact structure of the chart above. Not because I copied it, but because so many of us land in the same place after trying everything else.

Frequently Asked Questions

At what age should I start a chore chart?

Most parents start somewhere between ages 3 and 5, with one or two tiny tasks like putting toys in a bin. At that age, use pictures instead of words. A formal weekly grid with time blocks works well from about age 5 or 6, once your kid can read simple labels or follow icons.

Should I pay my kid for every chore?

I don't. In our house, daily responsibilities (making the bed, clearing plates) are unpaid because they're part of being in the family. Paid work is reserved for extras like washing the car or yard work. That separation keeps your kid from asking "how much will you pay me?" every time you ask for help.

What chores are appropriate for a 6-year-old?

A 6-year-old can typically make their bed (even if it's lumpy), clear their plate, put dirty clothes in the hamper, set the table, feed a pet with pre-measured food, and tidy toys for five minutes. Start with three and add more once those feel automatic. Expect "good enough," not perfect.

How do I make a chore chart reusable?

Laminate it, or slide it into a plastic sheet protector, and use dry-erase markers. Tape a marker to a string next to the chart so it never goes missing. Wipe it clean every Sunday and you're set for the whole week. One chart can last a full school year.

Should chores be tied to allowance?

There are good arguments both ways, and I changed my mind over time. My current approach: daily responsibilities are *not* tied to allowance, but extra jobs are paid. Your kid still gets a small weekly allowance (using the half-your-age rule as a starting point) to practice managing money, and they can earn more through the job board. If your family does it differently, that's okay. The key is that your kid knows the rules before the week begins.
