What Is a Roth IRA for Teens? (And Why Starting at 16 Changes Everything)

Okay, let's address the elephant in the room: you're probably thinking "retirement?! I'm still figuring out what I want to do this weekend." I get it. When I was 16, retirement felt about as relevant to my life as learning to use a fax machine. But here's the thing—what if I told you that the money you earn from your summer job or weekend shifts could turn into over a million dollars by the time you actually need it? Not through some sketchy scheme, but through a totally legal account that the IRS basically gives you for free. That's a Roth IRA, and honestly? It might be the most powerful financial tool you'll ever have access to.

This isn't another boring lecture about "saving for your future." This is real talk about a specific account that works differently for teenagers than it does for adults—and those differences are actually in your favor. Whether you're making money from babysitting, a part-time job at the mall, or freelance work, you might already qualify to open one. Let's break down exactly what a Roth IRA is, why starting as a teen is basically a financial cheat code, and the step-by-step process to actually get one set up. No finance degree required.

I didn't open a Roth IRA until I was 34—and when I ran the numbers on what I'd missed, I actually felt a little sick. My oldest is 14 now, and you better believe we opened her custodial Roth the week she got her first babysitting gig. The $400 she contributed that year could be worth $20,000+ by the time she retires. That's the math I wish someone had shown me at her age.

Does a 16-Year-Old Really Need a Retirement Account?

Short answer: need? Maybe not. Should you have one anyway? Absolutely yes.

Here's the deal. A Roth IRA isn't just a "retirement account" in the way your grandparents think about it. It's actually a super flexible investment account where your money grows completely tax-free. Like, forever. You put in money you've already paid taxes on (from your job), and then you never pay taxes on it again—not when it grows, not when you take it out decades from now.

Compare that to a regular investment account where you'd owe taxes every single year on your gains. Or a traditional IRA where you pay taxes when you withdraw. The Roth lets your money compound without the government taking a cut along the way.

But here's what really gets me excited: as a teenager, you're probably in the lowest tax bracket you'll ever be in. Like, ever. You might pay 10% or even 0% on your income right now. If you wait until you're 35 and making a solid salary, you could be paying 22% or more. By contributing now, you're locking in those low tax rates forever.

Plus—and this is huge—a Roth IRA isn't as locked up as people think. You can always withdraw your contributions (not the earnings, but the money you put in) without penalty. So it's not like this money disappears until you're 65. It's more like a savings account with superpowers.

Why It Matters: The Math That Will Blow Your Mind

Okay, I'm about to throw some numbers at you, and I promise they're worth paying attention to.

Let's say you're 16 and you contribute $3,000 to a Roth IRA this year. Just this one year. Then you completely forget about it and never add another dollar. Assuming an average 8% annual return (which is actually conservative based on historical stock market performance), here's what happens:

  • At age 25: $6,476
  • At age 35: $13,983
  • At age 45: $30,187
  • At age 55: $65,174
  • At age 65: $140,712

One contribution of $3,000 turned into over $140,000. Tax-free. From money you earned making smoothies or mowing lawns.

Now imagine you contribute $3,000 every year from age 16 to 22 (seven years of summer jobs and part-time work), then stop completely. That's $21,000 total that you put in. By age 65? You're looking at approximately $985,000. Nearly a million dollars from less than seven years of contributions you made as a teenager.

Your friend who waits until 30 to start? They'd need to contribute over $400,000 of their own money to end up in the same place. The difference isn't savings rate or investment skill—it's just time. This is compound interest doing its thing, and starting young is the only way to fully unlock it.

This is exactly why knowing how to invest even $100 as a teenager matters so much—a Roth IRA is where that $100 can do the most work over time.

Common Mistakes Parents (and Teens) Make With Roth IRAs

I see the same mistakes over and over again, and most of them are totally avoidable.

Mistake #1: Thinking You Need a "Real" Job

You don't need a W-2 from a big company. Babysitting money counts. Lawn mowing counts. Tutoring counts. Etsy sales count. As long as it's earned income (not allowance or investment gains), it qualifies. The CFPB emphasizes that any legitimate earned income works—you just need to be able to document it.

Mistake #2: Contributing More Than You Earned

For 2025-2026, the Roth IRA contribution limit is $7,000 per year—OR your total earned income, whichever is less. So if you made $3,200 this summer, your max contribution is $3,200. Not a penny more. The IRS does not play around with this rule.

Mistake #3: Just Letting the Money Sit in Cash

This one hurts my soul. Opening a Roth IRA and depositing money is only step one. That money then needs to be invested in something—usually index funds or target-date funds. I've seen people let their Roth contributions sit in cash for years, earning basically nothing. The account itself doesn't grow your money; the investments inside it do.

Mistake #4: Waiting for the "Perfect" Amount

You don't need to max out the contribution limit to start. $500 is fine. $100 is fine. Some brokerages have no minimum at all. Waiting until you have $7,000 sitting around means you're missing out on time in the market, which matters more than the amount.

Mistake #5: Not Documenting Cash Income

If you're earning money from gigs like babysitting or yard work, keep records. A simple spreadsheet works: date, what you did, who paid you, how much. If the IRS ever asks, you want proof that your contributions came from legitimate earned income.

What Actually Works: How to Open and Fund a Custodial Roth IRA

Here's the step-by-step process. It's actually way easier than you'd think.

Step 1: Confirm You Have Earned Income

This is the only real requirement. Did you get paid for work this year? Paycheck from an employer, cash from a side hustle, 1099 income from freelancing—any of these count. Allowance, birthday money, and investment returns do NOT count.

Step 2: Choose a Brokerage

Since you're under 18, you need a custodial Roth IRA. A parent or guardian will technically own the account until you reach adulthood. The good news? Most major brokerages offer these with no fees and no minimums:

  • Fidelity: $0 minimum, great app, offers a "Youth Account" option
  • Charles Schwab: $0 minimum, solid customer service
  • Vanguard: May require higher minimums for some funds, but great low-cost index funds

Personally? Fidelity tends to be the most teen-friendly in terms of interface and minimums.

Step 3: Gather Your Documents

You'll need:

  • Your Social Security number
  • Your parent's/guardian's info (SSN, address, etc.)
  • Your earned income information (not required to open, but good to have documented)

Step 4: Open the Account Online

This usually takes about 15 minutes. Your parent will fill out most of the paperwork since they're the custodian. You'll be listed as the beneficiary/minor account holder.

Step 5: Fund the Account

Transfer money from a checking or savings account. Remember: you can only contribute up to the amount you earned, maxing out at $7,000. Many families do a "match"—if you earned $3,000, your parents contribute $3,000 to the Roth IRA as a gift (totally legal, since you earned at least that much).

Step 6: Actually Invest the Money

Don't skip this! Once the cash lands in your account, you need to buy investments. For most teens, a simple total stock market index fund (like Fidelity's FZROX or Vanguard's VTI) is perfect. You can also choose a target-date fund for your expected retirement year (like a 2065 fund) if you want something even more hands-off.

Step 7: Set Up Auto-Contributions (Optional but Smart)

If you have regular income from a part-time job, set up automatic monthly contributions. Even $50/month adds up to $600/year—which could be worth $15,000+ by retirement.

Age-by-Age Breakdown: What to Do at 14, 16, and 18

Age 14-15: The Early Starter

At this age, your income is probably inconsistent—maybe some babysitting here, lawn work there. That's totally fine.

Action steps:

  • Start tracking any money you earn (date, amount, what you did)
  • Ask a parent about opening a custodial Roth IRA
  • Contribute whatever you can—even $200-$500 is meaningful at this age
  • Pick a simple total market index fund

Realistic goal: $500-$1,500 contributed

Age 16-17: The Part-Time Worker

This is when most teens get their first "official" job—fast food, retail, lifeguarding, etc. You're now earning regular paychecks with real documentation.

Action steps:

  • Open a custodial Roth IRA if you haven't already
  • Aim to contribute at least 25-50% of your summer earnings
  • Set up automatic investments if possible
  • Don't stress about maxing out—consistency beats perfection

Realistic goal: $1,500-$4,000 contributed annually

Age 18: The Transition Year

Big changes happen at 18. Your custodial account transfers to your control. You can now open accounts in your own name.

Action steps:

  • Contact your brokerage to transition the custodial account to a regular Roth IRA in your name
  • Update beneficiary information
  • If you're working or have a summer job before college, keep contributing
  • If you have college jobs (work-study counts!), continue your contributions

Realistic goal: $3,000-$7,000 contributed, plus maintaining existing investments

By the time you're heading to college or starting your career, you could easily have $10,000-$25,000 in your Roth IRA. That's a massive head start that most people don't get until their 30s (if ever).

What People Are Actually Wondering

I spend way too much time on Reddit reading questions from teens and parents trying to figure this stuff out. Here are real questions I've seen:

"My 16 year old has a summer job. Should I open a Roth IRA for her? She made about $3,200 this summer."

— r/personalfinance

My take: YES. This is literally the perfect scenario. She has documented earned income from a real job, which makes everything simple. She can contribute up to $3,200 (the full amount she earned). Even if she only puts in half—$1,600—that money has 50+ years to grow. At 8% average returns, that $1,600 becomes roughly $75,000 by age 65. From one summer of work. If the family can swing it, a parent "matching" her contribution (gifting her money to contribute since she has the earned income to qualify) would double that potential.

"Can a minor open a Roth IRA? My son is 15 and just started his first job at a grocery store."

— r/investing

My take: A minor can't open a regular Roth IRA, but they absolutely CAN have a custodial Roth IRA. The parent opens and manages the account until the teen turns 18 (or 21 in some states), at which point it transfers to full teen ownership. Fidelity, Schwab, and Vanguard all offer these. The process takes about 15 minutes online. His grocery store income is perfect—it's W-2 documented income, so there's zero ambiguity about whether it qualifies. Get that account open before the end of the year so he can contribute for this tax year.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a Roth IRA for teens?

A Roth IRA for teens is a retirement investment account where teenagers can contribute money they've earned from jobs or self-employment. Since minors can't open investment accounts independently, these are structured as "custodial" Roth IRAs—a parent or guardian manages the account until the teen reaches adulthood (18 or 21 depending on the state). The money grows tax-free, and withdrawals in retirement are also tax-free. It's one of the most powerful wealth-building tools available because teens have decades for their money to compound.

How much can a teenager contribute to a Roth IRA?

A teenager can contribute up to $7,000 per year (2025-2026 limit) OR their total earned income—whichever amount is less. For example, if your teen earned $2,500 from a summer job, they can only contribute $2,500. If they earned $10,000 from a part-time job throughout the year, they can contribute the maximum $7,000. The IRS is strict about this: contributions cannot exceed documented earned income, and that income must come from work (not allowance, gifts, or investment returns).

Does my teen need earned income to open a Roth IRA?

Yes, absolutely. This is the most important rule for Roth IRAs at any age. Your teen must have earned income—money paid for work they performed. This includes W-2 income from an employer (fast food, retail, lifeguarding), 1099 income from freelancing or gig work, and documented cash income from jobs like babysitting or lawn care. What doesn't count: allowance, monetary gifts, investment earnings, or inheritance. Keep records of all income sources, especially cash payments, in case the IRS ever asks for documentation.

What's the difference between a custodial Roth IRA and a regular Roth IRA?

The main difference is who controls the account. A custodial Roth IRA is managed by a parent or guardian on behalf of the minor. The adult makes investment decisions and controls transactions, but the account belongs to the teen (it's in their name and uses their Social Security number). A regular Roth IRA is opened and fully controlled by an adult. The contribution rules, tax benefits, and investment options are identical—the only difference is the management structure required because minors can't enter into financial contracts independently.

What happens to a custodial Roth IRA when my teen turns 18?

When your teen reaches the age of majority (18 in most states, 21 in a few), the custodial Roth IRA transfers to their full ownership and control. This typically requires some paperwork with your brokerage to convert the custodial account to a regular Roth IRA in the now-adult's name. The transfer doesn't trigger any taxes or penalties—it's a simple administrative change. All the money, investments, and contribution history remain intact. Your teen then has complete control over investment decisions, contributions, and withdrawals going forward.
