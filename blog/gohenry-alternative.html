GoHenry Alternative: Best Kids Debit Card Apps for 2026

My daughter came home from school one afternoon and announced she wanted to buy her own birthday present — with her own money. She was nine. And I had absolutely no system for her to do that.

We'd been using a cash envelope on the fridge like it was 1987. I knew I needed a kids debit card app, but when I searched "GoHenry" — the app a few other parents had recommended — I kept landing on confusing redirects and "Acorns Early" branding I didn't recognize. Turns out, there's a whole story there. And once I figured it out, I actually liked what I found.

Wait — What Actually Happened to GoHenry?

GoHenry was one of the first purpose-built kids debit card apps to hit the US market, and for a while, it was the go-to recommendation in every parenting Facebook group. It let parents load money onto a card, set spending rules, and pay allowance automatically. Simple, solid, popular.

Then in 2023, Acorns acquired GoHenry and eventually folded it into their existing kids product, rebranding the whole thing as Acorns Early. If you had a GoHenry account, it migrated over. If you're searching for GoHenry alternatives now, Acorns Early is essentially what GoHenry became — just with more features layered on top, including real investing for kids.

So what should you actually look for in a kids money app in 2026? A few things matter more than people think:

  • Real debit card (Visa or Mastercard) your kid can use in stores and online
  • Parent controls — spending limits, merchant blocking, instant notifications
  • Allowance automation — you shouldn't have to manually send $5 every Saturday
  • Financial education built in — not just a card, but a learning tool
  • Transparent fees — some apps charge $5–$10/month, others are free
  • Age appropriateness — a 7-year-old and a 16-year-old need different features

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) recommends that kids start handling real money decisions as early as age 6 — because that's when the habits form. An app makes that way more practical than a cash envelope on the fridge (yes, I retired mine).

The Best GoHenry Alternatives for 2026

Six apps are worth your time. I've broken them down honestly — fees, age ranges, and what they're actually good at.

1. Acorns Early — Best Overall GoHenry Alternative

This is what GoHenry became, and it's genuinely better than the original. Acorns Early gives your kid a debit card for kids, a spending account, and — this is the part that got me — the ability to actually invest money into a real portfolio. My 9-year-old has $47 invested in a diversified ETF portfolio right now. That's not a simulation. That's real.

  • Age range: 6–18
  • Cost: Included with Acorns subscription ($3/month for Personal, $5/month for Family — covers unlimited kids)
  • Key features: Debit card, automated allowance, chores, savings goals, real investment account, parent controls
  • Best for: Parents who want a debit card AND want to start building their kid's investment habit early

The Family plan at $5/month covering unlimited kids is genuinely good value. If you have two or more kids, you're paying less per child than most competing apps charge for one. And GoHenry used to charge $4.99 per child — so this is actually a better deal for most families.

2. Chase First Banking — Best Free Option

If paying a monthly fee makes you twitch, Chase First Banking is worth a hard look. It's completely free — no monthly fee, no card fee — as long as you have a Chase checking account. The debit card for kids comes with parent-set spending limits, transfer controls, and real-time notifications.

  • Age range: 6–17
  • Cost: Free (requires parent Chase checking account)
  • Key features: Spending limits by category, ATM access, allowance automation, parent dashboard
  • Best for: Chase customers who want zero-cost basics

The limitation is what's missing: no investing, no financial literacy content, no chores tracker. It's a clean, functional kids debit card — not a teaching tool. If your kid is under 10 and you just want them to practice spending real money, that's fine. If you want more, keep reading.

3. Capital One MONEY — Runner-Up Free Pick

Capital One MONEY is another fee-free option that doesn't require you to already be a Capital One customer — which gives it a leg up on Chase for a lot of families. Your kid gets their own login, can set savings goals, and see their balance in real time.

  • Age range: 8–18
  • Cost: Free
  • Key features: Joint account model (parent co-owns), savings goals, real-time balance, no fees
  • Best for: Families who aren't Chase customers and want a free starter card

No chore tracking, no investing, no financial education content. But zero dollars a month is zero dollars a month, and for an 8-year-old learning to not overspend on Roblox, it works.

4. Step — Best for Teens Building Credit

Step flips the script: instead of a debit card, it's a secured credit card for teens that functions like a debit card. Every purchase is backed by the balance in the account, but it reports to credit bureaus — meaning your 13-year-old can be building a credit history right now.

  • Age range: 13–18 (under 18 requires parent sponsor)
  • Cost: Free (Step Plus is $3.99/month for added perks)
  • Key features: Secured credit card, credit building, crypto access (optional), peer-to-peer transfers
  • Best for: Teens 13+ whose parents want them building credit before college

I didn't know credit-building for minors was even possible until I was 34. Genuinely wish I'd had this conversation with my parents. The Next Gen Personal Finance team has written extensively about why early credit building matters — it's worth 5 minutes of your time.

5. BusyKid — Best for Chore-Focused Families

BusyKid is built around chores first, spending second. Your kid completes tasks, earns their allowance automatically on Fridays, and can choose to spend, save, share, or invest their earnings. The structure is exactly what some kids need.

  • Age range: 5–17
  • Cost: $4/month or $38.99/year (covers up to 5 kids)
  • Key features: Chore assignment and tracking, automated Friday paydays, investing option, charitable giving feature, Visa spend card
  • Best for: Families who want chores tied directly to earnings

The Friday payday feature is low-key brilliant. It mirrors how the real world works — you do the work, then you get paid on a schedule. That rhythm builds expectation in a healthy way. Check out our post on the 3-jar system for kids — BusyKid's spend/save/share model maps almost perfectly onto it.

6. FamZoo — Best for Financial Structure Nerds

FamZoo is not the prettiest app. The interface looks like it was designed in 2014, because it kind of was. But the functionality underneath is remarkable — it's the most customizable kids money app on this list.

  • Age range: Any age (parent-defined)
  • Cost: $5.99/month or $2.50/month when paid 2 years upfront
  • Key features: IOU accounts for younger kids, prepaid cards, parent-paid interest (you set the rate), loan tracking, bill splitting, chore management
  • Best for: Parents who want to run a "family bank" with real financial mechanics

The parent-paid interest feature is my favorite thing in kids fintech. You tell FamZoo to pay your kid 3% monthly interest on their savings balance — and you cover it yourself. Your kid learns compound interest by experiencing it. That is actual financial education, not a worksheet.

I ran FamZoo for three months before switching to Acorns Early, mostly because my kids didn't love the interface — but I still think FamZoo is one of the most underrated tools in this space, especially for parents who want their kids to understand how borrowing and interest actually work before they turn 18.

Your Questions About Kids Debit Card Apps, Answered

What age can a kid get a debit card?

Most kids debit card apps start at age 6, though some like BusyKid go as young as 5. Traditional banks typically require kids to be at least 13 for a debit card tied to a checking account. Apps like Acorns Early, Chase First Banking, and FamZoo all have options starting at 6 because they operate as custodial or prepaid accounts — meaning the parent is the legal account holder.

Is Acorns Early the same as GoHenry?

Essentially, yes — Acorns acquired GoHenry and migrated its features into Acorns Early. Existing GoHenry accounts moved over automatically. Acorns Early has everything GoHenry had (debit card, parent controls, allowance automation) plus the addition of real investing for kids, which GoHenry didn't offer. It's a meaningful upgrade, not just a rebrand.

Are kids debit card apps safe?

The reputable ones — Acorns Early, Chase First Banking, Capital One MONEY — are FDIC-insured, meaning your kid's balance is protected up to $250,000 just like an adult bank account. Prepaid card apps like FamZoo and BusyKid have FDIC pass-through insurance through their banking partners. Always check that any app you use lists FDIC insurance clearly. Investopedia has a clear breakdown of how FDIC insurance works if you want the full picture.

Can my kid use a kids debit card online?

Yes — every card on this list is a Visa or Mastercard that works online, in stores, and at most ATMs. The difference is what the parent controls allow. With Acorns Early, Chase First Banking, and BusyKid, you can block specific merchant categories (so no surprise Roblox purchases at midnight). That merchant-blocking feature alone is worth the subscription for a lot of parents.

What's the best kids money app if I have multiple kids?

Acorns Early (Family plan at $5/month for unlimited kids) and BusyKid ($38.99/year for up to 5 kids) are the two best values for multi-kid households. Chase First Banking is technically free for each kid, but requires a separate account setup per child. FamZoo's flat family fee also covers multiple kids, which makes its $2.50/month annual prepay option very reasonable. Also — if your kids are old enough to be thinking about ways to earn their own money, check out our guide on how to make money as a kid — some of those ideas pair really well with having their own account to deposit into.

Do any of these apps teach actual financial concepts?

Acorns Early has in-app financial education content. BusyKid has a giving/saving/spending framework that mirrors real-world money management. FamZoo is essentially a simulation of a family bank, including loans and interest. Step has content around credit scores specifically for teens. Chase First Banking and Capital One MONEY are more bare-bones — they give your kid a card and a balance, but the financial education has to come from you. Which is fine. That's kind of what dinner table conversations are for.
