Why We Started Using Routine Charts (And Why I Wish I'd Started Sooner)

Here's what our mornings looked like before routine charts: I'd wake up my 7-year-old at 7am. By 7:45am, I'd reminded her to brush her teeth three times, asked about her backpack twice, and we were both frazzled before we even got to the car.

The breaking point? She forgot her lunch box four days in one week.

I wasn't mad at her—she's a kid. But I was exhausted from being the human alarm system for every single task. That's when my friend showed me her laminated morning chart with little velcro checkmarks.

It felt almost too simple. But we made one that weekend—just 6 tasks, clipart images, and a pack of star stickers from Target. Monday morning, my daughter checked off "brush teeth" without me saying a word.

I nearly cried into my coffee.

What Is a Daily Routine Chart? (And Why It Actually Works)

A daily routine chart is exactly what it sounds like: a visual list of tasks your kid needs to do at specific times of day. Morning routine, after-school routine, bedtime routine—whatever works for your family.

The magic isn't the chart itself. It's that your kid can see what comes next without asking you.

According to the CDC's research on childhood development, predictable routines help kids feel secure and build independence. When kids know what to expect, they're less likely to resist—and more likely to do tasks on their own.

The visual part matters too. My 9-year-old is a strong reader, but pictures work faster than words when he's half-asleep at 6:45am. Kids ages 5-10 are still developing executive function skills (planning, time management, task initiation). A chart does some of that heavy lifting for them.

Think of it like this: you probably have a mental checklist before you leave for work. Kids need an external version of that checklist until their brains are ready to build their own.

How to Make Your Own Daily Routine Chart (Step-by-Step)

You don't need to be crafty. You don't need a laminator (though I did eventually get one and I love it). Here's exactly how to make a chart that works.

Step 1: Pick ONE routine to start

Don't do morning, after-school, AND bedtime all at once. Pick whichever routine causes the most chaos in your house right now. For us, it was mornings.

You can add more charts later. Start small.

Step 2: List 5-8 tasks, max

Write down every single thing your kid needs to do during that routine. Then cut it down to the essentials—the non-negotiables.

Our first morning chart had: wake up, brush teeth, get dressed, eat breakfast, put on shoes, grab backpack. That's it. No "make bed" or "feed the dog" yet. Just the stuff that HAS to happen.

Step 3: Add pictures for each task

Even if your kid can read, pictures make it faster to scan. I used free clipart from Google Images and printed them in color. You can also draw them—my stick figures were terrible but my kids loved them.

For younger kids (ages 3-6), pictures are essential. For older kids (7-10), they still help, especially in the morning when brains are slow.

Step 4: Choose how they'll mark completion

Stickers, checkmarks with dry-erase markers, velcro dots, clothespins—anything tactile works. My daughter loves stickers. My son prefers checking boxes with a marker because he thinks stickers are "babyish" now.

Let your kid pick. The more ownership they have, the more they'll use it.

Step 5: Put it where they'll see it

We stuck ours on the bathroom mirror with Command strips. It's the first thing she sees when she stumbles in to brush her teeth.

Other parents I know use the fridge, the back of the bedroom door, or a clipboard hanging on the wall. Wherever your kid naturally goes during that routine.

What to Put on a Daily Routine Chart by Age

The tasks should match what your kid can actually do independently (or almost independently). Here's what works at different ages.

Ages 3-5: Keep it SHORT

3-4 tasks max. Focus on the absolute basics: brush teeth, get dressed, eat breakfast. That's honestly enough.

At this age, you're still helping with most of it. The chart is teaching them the order of the routine, not full independence. Pictures are critical—use big, simple images.

Morning example: brush teeth, get dressed, eat breakfast.
Bedtime example: put on pajamas, brush teeth, pick a book.

Ages 6-8: Add responsibility

5-7 tasks. Now you can add things like "pack backpack" or "put dishes in sink." Kids this age can handle a few steps without you standing there.

This is when routine charts really shine. Your kid wants to feel independent, but they're not quite ready to remember everything on their own. The chart bridges that gap.

Morning example: wake up, brush teeth, get dressed, eat breakfast, pack backpack, put on shoes.
Bedtime example: put on pajamas, brush teeth, pack tomorrow's backpack, lay out clothes, read for 15 minutes.

Ages 9-12: Include ownership tasks

6-8 tasks. Add stuff that builds responsibility: make bed, feed pets, check homework folder, set out tomorrow's clothes.

At this age, some kids won't want a "babyish" chart. That's fine—let them design it. My son made his in Google Docs with checkboxes. It's not cute, but it's his, and he uses it.

You can also tie the routine chart to systems like our weekly chore chart or even money management tools if your kid earns allowance for extra tasks.

Common Mistakes Parents Make with Routine Charts (And How to Fix Them)

I've made every single one of these mistakes. Here's what doesn't work—and what does.

Mistake #1: Making the chart too long

If your chart has 12 tasks, your kid will give up before breakfast. I learned this the hard way when I added "make bed," "water plants," and "write in gratitude journal" to our morning chart.

My daughter looked at it and said, "This is too much, Mom."

She was right. Stick to 5-8 tasks. If something isn't critical, save it for another routine or drop it entirely.

Mistake #2: Expecting perfection immediately

The first week, your kid will probably still forget tasks. They'll skip steps. They'll need reminders. That's normal.

The chart isn't magic—it's a tool. It took us about two weeks before my daughter stopped needing me to say "check your chart." Be patient.

Mistake #3: Not updating it as they grow

What worked at age 6 won't work at age 9. Every 6-12 months, sit down with your kid and ask: what's easy now? What should we add?

My son recently asked to remove "get dressed" from his chart because "I obviously know to do that, Mom." Fair. We replaced it with "take vitamins," which he actually forgets.

Mistake #4: Using it as a punishment tool

Don't take away privileges if your kid doesn't complete the chart perfectly. That makes it stressful instead of helpful.

The chart is a support tool, not a test. If your kid consistently skips a task, ask why. Maybe it's too hard, in the wrong order, or not actually necessary.

Mistake #5: Making it parent-directed instead of kid-owned

If you're the only one who cares about the chart, it won't work. Let your kid help make it. Let them choose stickers or colors or how to mark tasks.

The more it feels like theirs, the more they'll use it.

Free Printable Daily Routine Chart: What to Look for and How to Use It

You can absolutely make your own from scratch (I did for the first year). But printable templates save a ton of time, especially if you're not artsy.

Here's what to look for in a good printable routine chart:

Clear, simple images. Clipart is fine, but it should be easy to understand at a glance. If your 6-year-old has to ask what a picture means, it's too complicated.

Editable or customizable. The best printables let you choose which tasks to include. Every family is different—your morning routine might include "feed the cat" while mine doesn't.

Space for your kid to mark completion. Whether it's checkboxes, star spaces, or velcro dots, make sure there's a clear way for your kid to show they finished a task.

Age-appropriate design. A chart for a 4-year-old should have big pictures and minimal text. A chart for a 10-year-old can be more streamlined and text-based.

Our free printable kit includes routine charts for morning and bedtime, designed for ages 5-8. You can print them as-is or customize the tasks to fit your family.

Once you print it, laminate it if you can (or slide it into a page protector). That way your kid can use dry-erase markers or stickers over and over without wasting paper.

Pro tip: print two copies. One for mornings, one for bedtime. Hang them in different spots so your kid isn't running back and forth to the same chart all day.

What age should I start using a daily routine chart?

You can start as early as age 3, but keep it simple—just 3-4 tasks with big pictures. Ages 5-8 is when routine charts really take off because kids want independence but still need structure. My daughter started using one at age 7 and it made mornings so much easier.

How long does it take for a routine chart to work?

Give it at least two weeks. The first few days, you'll still need to remind your kid to check the chart. By week two, most kids start using it on their own. My son took about 10 days before he stopped asking me what came next. Be patient and celebrate small wins.

Should I offer rewards for completing the routine chart?

You don't have to, but it can help in the beginning. We did stickers for a month—after a full week of completed charts, my daughter got to pick a family movie night. Now the routine itself is the reward because mornings are calmer. Keep rewards simple and phase them out once the habit sticks.

What if my kid refuses to use the chart?

Ask why. Sometimes the chart is too long, too babyish, or in the wrong spot. Let your kid help redesign it. My son hated our first chart because it had "cutesy" pictures. We remade it together in black and white with checkboxes, and suddenly he was on board. Kid buy-in is everything.

Can I use the same chart for multiple kids?

You can, but I don't recommend it—especially if they're different ages. Each kid needs tasks that match their abilities. My 7-year-old's chart had "put on shoes." My 9-year-old's included "check homework folder." Make separate charts and let each kid personalize theirs. It's worth the extra five minutes.

Do routine charts work for kids with ADHD or autism?

Yes—often even better than for neurotypical kids. Visual schedules are a evidence-based tool recommended by experts at Understood.org for kids with ADHD, autism, and executive function challenges. Keep the chart consistent, use clear images, and consider adding time estimates for each task. Talk to your child's therapist or teacher for specific adaptations that might help.
