Custodial Brokerage Account: A Parent's Guide to Investing for Kids

Maya Hartwell Parent-Tested ✓

Former math teacher · Mom of two · Editor, WealthSprout

📅 September 12, 2026 · 10 min read

When my 14-year-old asked if he could buy stock in the company that makes his favorite gaming headphones, I froze. Not because it was a bad idea — it was actually a great one. I froze because I didn't know how to make it happen legally. I didn't even know what a custodial brokerage account was until I was 34.

Turns out, opening a custodial brokerage account for your kid is one of the smartest money moves a parent can make. It's not just about teaching investing. It's about giving your kid a front-row seat to compound interest while they're still young enough for time to do the heavy lifting. Here's everything I wish I knew five years earlier.

I didn't know custodial accounts existed until my son was 13. I kept his savings in a regular bank account earning 0.01% interest. Looking back, that was five years of compound growth we just missed. If you're reading this and your kid is under 14, you're already ahead of where I was.

What Is a Custodial Brokerage Account, Really?

A custodial brokerage account is an investment account that an adult opens and manages on behalf of a minor. The money in the account legally belongs to the child, but the adult custodian — usually a parent — controls the investments and withdrawals until the child reaches the age of majority, typically 18 or 21 depending on your state.

These accounts fall under two main types: UTMA (Uniform Transfers to Minors Act) and UGMA (Uniform Gifts to Minors Act). UTMA is the more common version today because it allows a broader range of assets, including real estate and intellectual property. For most families, the practical difference is minimal — you'll likely open a UTMA.

Unlike a regular bank account for a child, a custodial brokerage account lets your kid own actual investments: stocks, bonds, ETFs, and mutual funds. It's the difference between parking cash and growing it.

Why a Custodial Account Matters More Than You Think

According to Next Gen Personal Finance (NGPF), teens who start investing even small amounts before age 18 develop stronger financial confidence and significantly better long-term wealth habits than those who wait until their twenties.

Here's the math that convinced me: If your kid invests $50 per month starting at age 14, and earns an average 8% annual return, they'll have roughly $87,000 by age 35. If they wait until 25 to start? They'd need to invest $175 per month to reach the same amount. Time is the asset your kid has that you don't.

My daughter opened her custodial account at 13. She bought three shares of a company she actually understood. Six months later, she watched those shares pay a dividend. It was $1.42. She was thrilled. Not because of the amount, but because she finally understood that owning a piece of a business means you get a slice of the profits. That's a lesson no lecture can match.

Plus, according to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB), early exposure to financial markets helps kids develop better decision-making skills around risk, patience, and long-term thinking. The account itself becomes the teacher.

The Mistakes Parents Make (And I Made Too)

I have opened custodial accounts for both my kids, and I messed up at least twice along the way. Here are the traps most parents fall into:

Mistake 1: Treating It Like a Savings Account

A custodial brokerage account is not a place to park birthday money and forget about it. If the money just sits in cash, you're missing the entire point. The account only works if you actually invest the funds. That means choosing ETFs, index funds, or individual stocks — not letting cash sit idle earning nothing.

Mistake 2: Trying to Pick the Next Big Stock

I get the temptation. You want your kid to have a fun story about how they bought the next Apple at age 12. But here's the truth: most individual stock pickers underperform the market. According to Investopedia, studies consistently show that low-cost index funds outperform actively managed portfolios over long time horizons. Teach your kid to buy the whole market, not bet on a single company.

Mistake 3: Forgetting the Money Is Legally Theirs

This is the biggest shock for most parents. Once you put money into a custodial account, it legally belongs to your kid. You can't take it back. You can't use it for your own expenses. You can't change your mind and transfer it to a sibling. The money is theirs, and at the age of majority, they control it completely. Plan accordingly.

Mistake 4: Ignoring the Tax Rules

Custodial accounts are subject to something called the "kiddie tax." For 2026, the first $1,300 of unearned income is tax-free. The next $1,300 is taxed at the child's rate. Anything above $2,600 is taxed at the parents' marginal rate. For most families with small accounts, this won't matter much. But if the account grows significantly, consult a tax professional.

Mistake 5: Waiting for the "Right Time"

There is no perfect time to start. I hear parents say, "I'll wait until they understand the stock market better." The best way to understand the stock market is to participate in it with real money — even a tiny amount. Start with $100. Let them watch it move. The learning happens in the doing.

How to Open a Custodial Brokerage Account: Step-by-Step

After researching dozens of platforms and opening accounts for my own kids, here's the setup that actually works:

Step 1: Choose the Right Brokerage

Look for a platform that offers custodial accounts with no minimum balance, low or zero commissions, and a user-friendly interface. Most major brokerages — Fidelity, Schwab, Vanguard, E*Trade — offer custodial accounts. Some newer platforms like Acorns and Greenlight also have investing features for kids, though they may have monthly fees.

My recommendation: start with a traditional brokerage for the broadest investment options and lowest fees.

Step 2: Gather Required Documents

You'll need:

  • Your government-issued ID
  • Your Social Security number
  • Your child's Social Security number
  • Proof of address
  • Your bank account information for funding

The application takes about 10 minutes online. Approval is usually instant.

Step 3: Fund the Account

Start small. $100 is enough to buy a few shares of a broad-market ETF. The goal is not to fund a retirement — it's to start the habit. You can set up automatic monthly transfers, which is what we do in our house. My kids each get $25 per month into their accounts, funded from their allowance and side hustle earnings.

If your kid is earning money from a side hustle or part-time job, that opens up even more possibilities. Check out our how to make money as a kid guide for real ways teens can start earning.

Step 4: Make the First Investment Together

Don't invest for them — invest with them. Sit down together, look at a few ETFs, and let them choose. Explain what an ETF is: a basket of stocks that gives you exposure to hundreds of companies at once. My son chose VOO (Vanguard S&P 500 ETF) because he liked that it owned a piece of every major company he'd heard of.

Step 5: Review the Account Monthly

Make it a ritual. Once a month, pull up the account together. Talk about what went up, what went down, and why. Don't obsess over short-term fluctuations. The goal is awareness, not day-trading. Ask: "What do you notice?" Let them lead the observation.

What Should Your Kid Actually Invest In?

This is the question I get most often, and my answer is boring on purpose: low-cost index funds and ETFs.

Here's why. According to Investopedia, the S&P 500 has returned an average of about 10% annually before inflation over the past century. A single share of VOO or VTI gives your kid exposure to the entire U.S. stock market. It's diversified, it's low-cost (expense ratio under 0.05%), and it requires zero stock-picking skill.

If your kid is genuinely interested in individual stocks, let them allocate 10-20% of the account to companies they know and understand. My daughter owns a few shares of a streaming company and a gaming company. The rest is in index funds. This gives her the excitement of ownership without the risk of betting everything on one stock.

Avoid complex products: no options, no margin, no leveraged ETFs. These are gambling tools, not investing tools. Keep it simple. The lesson is more important than the returns.

What to Expect at Every Age

Ages 5-8: Plant the Seed

At this age, your kid is not ready for a brokerage account. But they are ready to learn the concept of owning something that grows. Start with the 3-jar system for kids: spend, save, give. The "save" jar is their first experience with delayed gratification. That's the mental foundation they'll need before they ever see a stock chart.

Ages 9-13: Show, Don't Tell

This is the sweet spot to open the account. Kids this age can understand that a stock is a piece of a company. Let them pick one or two companies they know. Let them watch the price move. The goal is not to make money — it's to make the abstract concrete. My 11-year-old checks his account balance more often than his video game stats. That's the point.

Ages 14-18: Real Money, Real Decisions

By high school, your kid should be managing a real account with real money from jobs, side hustles, or structured contributions from you. This is where the stakes get higher and the lessons get sharper. They need to understand that a 20% drop in the market is normal, not a reason to panic sell. They need to understand that holding for years is how wealth is actually built.

Encourage them to research companies. Ask them to explain why they want to buy something. If they can't explain it in a sentence, they don't understand it well enough to own it.

What Parents Are Actually Asking

I spend a lot of time reading what parents are genuinely confused about. Here's what's coming up again and again:

"I want to open a brokerage account for my 10-year-old but I have no idea where to start. What platform should I use?"

— via Reddit

Start with a major brokerage like Fidelity, Schwab, or Vanguard. They all offer UTMA/UGMA accounts with no minimums and zero commissions on most trades. The interface might feel a bit dry compared to apps like Robinhood, but that's actually a feature — you want your kid learning investing, not gamified trading.

"Can I use a custodial account to save for college? Or is a 529 better?"

— via Quora

A 529 plan is generally better for college savings because of the tax advantages: earnings grow tax-free if used for qualified education expenses. A custodial brokerage account is better for general wealth-building and teaching investing. The money in a custodial account can be used for anything once the child reaches majority age. Many families do both — 529 for college, custodial account for investing education.

"What happens to the account when my kid turns 18? Do they just get all the money and blow it?"

— via Reddit

At the age of majority (18 or 21 depending on your state), the account transfers fully to your kid. You lose all control. The best defense against irresponsible spending is education. If your kid has been involved in the account decisions all along — watching investments grow, understanding why you chose index funds, experiencing market drops — they are far less likely to blow it. The account isn't just a bucket of money. It's a decade of financial education, compressed into a brokerage statement.
