Checking Account for Kids: When They're Ready and How to Set It Up

It's about readiness. And readiness looks wildly different at 7 versus 10 versus 14.

I opened my first checking account for my daughter when she was 9. My son? He wasn't ready until 11. Same household, same financial values, completely different timelines. And that's exactly how it should be.

This isn't about hitting some arbitrary milestone. It's about watching your kid demonstrate they can handle basic money concepts, then giving them a real-world tool to practice with. A checking account is like training wheels for financial independence — but only if they're actually ready to pedal.

So let's figure out if your kid is ready, how to have that first conversation, and exactly how to set up an account that teaches without overwhelming.

When Are They Actually Ready?

Most banks will let you open a checking account for kids as young as 6. That doesn't mean you should.

Here's what I look for before I even consider it:

They can do basic addition and subtraction in their head. Not with a calculator. Not with their fingers. If they can't calculate $20 - $8.50 without help, they're not ready to track a checking account balance.

They understand delayed gratification. If they get $10 and immediately want to spend $10, a checking account won't teach them anything. They need to have demonstrated — even once — that they can save money toward a goal and actually reach it.

They have a regular source of money. Whether it's allowance, birthday money they're allowed to keep, or earnings from age-appropriate work, they need incoming funds to manage. A checking account with $0 forever is pointless.

They've asked questions about how you pay for things. Curiosity is the green light. If your kid has noticed you use a card instead of cash and asked why, or wondered where your money "lives," they're starting to think about banking.

If your kid checks all four boxes, they're probably ready. If they're missing two or more, wait six months and reassess.

Age-by-Age Breakdown: What They Can Handle

Ages 6-7: Probably Too Early (But Not Always)

At this age, most kids are still learning to count money. A checking account is abstract — they can't see the money, touch it, or count it. That makes it really hard to understand.

What they CAN handle: A physical money system (three jars, piggy banks, envelopes). Learning to recognize bills and coins. Understanding that things cost different amounts.

When it MIGHT work: If your 6- or 7-year-old is genuinely advanced with math, has been managing physical money for a year+, and is begging to have a card "like Mom," you can try it. Just expect to do every transaction together for at least a year.

Ages 8-10: The Sweet Spot for Most Kids

This is when most kids are ready. They can do mental math, understand that a debit card pulls from a "bucket" of money somewhere else, and feel proud of having something "official."

What they CAN handle: Checking their balance weekly with you. Making small purchases with parental approval (and you watching). Understanding that spending $10 means they now have $10 less.

What they CAN'T handle yet: Managing their account independently. Remembering to check their balance before spending. Understanding fees or declined transactions without your help.

Best account type: Joint checking account with heavy parental controls. You get alerts for every transaction. They get a debit card but can only use it with permission.

Ages 11-13: Building Independence

This is where it gets interesting. Your kid wants more autonomy. A checking account becomes a tool for teaching them how to manage it without you hovering.

What they CAN handle: Checking their own balance (with occasional reminders). Making small purchases without asking first, as long as they stay under a spending limit. Understanding that they need to track what they spend.

What they're learning: What happens when they miscalculate and don't have enough for something they want. How to decide between spending now vs. saving for something bigger. Why you check your balance before you go to the store.

Best account type: Joint checking account with parental monitoring but fewer restrictions. Set a weekly spending limit ($10-20) and let them manage within that boundary.

Ages 14-17: Near-Adult Money Management

At this age, a checking account isn't just educational — it's practical. If your teen has a job, they need somewhere to deposit their paycheck. If they're driving, they need to be able to buy gas.

What they CAN handle: Full responsibility for tracking their balance. Managing income from a job. Making purchase decisions without your input. Understanding consequences (overdraft fees, declined cards).

What they're learning: How to budget real money for real expenses. What it feels like to run out of funds and have to wait until the next paycheck. How to avoid fees and manage their account like an adult.

Best account type: Joint checking account with minimal parental intervention, or a teen checking account with investing features (like Fidelity Youth Account). You still have oversight, but you're mostly hands-off unless something goes sideways.

How to Start the Conversation

Don't just announce, "We're opening you a checking account!" and expect excitement. For most kids, banking is abstract and boring until you connect it to something they actually care about.

Here are three scripts I've used (and seen work with other parents):

For Younger Kids (Ages 8-10):

"You've been doing such a great job saving your allowance. I think you're ready for the next step: your own bank account with a debit card. That means your money will be kept safe at a bank, and you'll have a card you can use to spend it — kind of like mine. Want to try it?"

Keep it simple. Emphasize that it's an upgrade, a sign they're growing up. Let them ask questions.

For Middle Schoolers (Ages 11-13):

"I've noticed you're getting better at managing your money. I think it's time you had your own checking account. Here's how it works: the bank keeps your money safe, you get a card to spend it, and you can check your balance online anytime. I'll still see what you're spending, but you'll have more freedom to make your own choices. Sound good?"

At this age, "freedom" is the magic word. Frame it as increased independence, not increased surveillance.

For Teens (Ages 14-17):

"You're going to need a checking account, probably sooner than you think. If you get a job, you'll need somewhere for your paycheck to go. If you're buying stuff online or going out with friends, having your own account makes everything easier. Let's set one up this week so you know how to use it before you actually need it."

For teens, the conversation is practical, not aspirational. They're old enough to understand that this is a life skill, not a reward.

What to Expect When You Have the Conversation

They might not care. That's okay. Not every kid is going to be pumped about banking. Open the account anyway. The excitement comes later when they use it for something they actually want.

They might be worried. Some kids stress about "doing it wrong" or "losing the money." Reassure them: you're doing this together, you'll help them every step of the way, and the money is insured (which means it's protected even if something goes wrong with the bank).

They might have a million questions. Good! That's engagement. Answer every single one, even if it takes 30 minutes. Curiosity is the foundation of financial literacy.

Choosing the Right Checking Account

Not all kids' checking accounts are created equal. Some have great apps but terrible fees. Some are fee-free but require you to bank there too. Here's what I recommend based on your situation:

Best for Younger Kids (Ages 8-10): Chase First Banking

Age range: 6-17
Monthly fee: $0
Parent requirement: You must have a Chase checking account
What I like: The app is parent-controlled and intuitive. You can set spending limits, block certain merchants, and get real-time alerts. Your kid gets a debit card but can't use it without you knowing immediately.

What I don't like: If you don't already bank with Chase, you'll need to open an account. That's an extra step (though Chase's adult checking accounts are solid, so it's not a dealbreaker).

Best for: Families who want tight parental controls and a big-bank app experience.

Best for Maximum Flexibility: Capital One MONEY Teen

Age range: 8+
Monthly fee: $0
Parent requirement: You don't need a Capital One account
What I like: This is the most accessible option. No parent account required. No fees, no minimums, and your kid gets access to 70,000+ fee-free ATMs. The parental controls are strong but not overbearing.

What I don't like: The app is fine, but not as polished as Chase's.

Best for: Families who want a no-strings-attached, fee-free option and don't already have a preferred bank.

Best for Teens Who Want to Invest: Fidelity Youth Account

Age range: 13-17
Monthly fee: $0
Parent requirement: You must have a Fidelity account
What I like: This isn't just a checking account — it's a checking + investing account. Your teen can buy stocks, ETFs, and mutual funds with no commissions. If your kid is interested in investing or has earned money they want to grow, this is the move.

What I don't like: If your teen just wants to manage their allowance and buy snacks, this is overkill. The investing features are wasted on kids who aren't interested.

Best for: Teens who are money-curious, have income from a job, or have expressed interest in learning about investing.

Best for Parental Control Enthusiasts: Greenlight

Age range: Any age (parent decides)
Monthly fee: $5.99-$14.98/month depending on plan
Parent requirement: None
What I like: Greenlight isn't a bank — it's an app with a debit card attached. You control everything: spending limits, store restrictions, chore-based allowance automation, savings goals, investing options (on higher-tier plans). The parental dashboard is the best I've seen.

What I don't like: It's a subscription. For one kid, you're paying $72-$180/year. If you have multiple kids, the family plan makes more sense, but it's still a recurring cost.

Best for: Parents who want maximum control, automation, and gamification. Also great if you're homeschooling and want financial literacy built into the app itself.

Best for Local, Personal Service: Your Credit Union

Age range: Varies (usually 6-12+ with parental joint account)
Monthly fee: Almost always $0
Parent requirement: You'll need to be a member
What I like: Credit unions are nonprofit, so they tend to have zero fees, better interest rates, and genuine customer service. Many offer financial literacy programs for kids. If something goes wrong, you can walk into a branch and talk to a human who knows your name.

What I don't like: The apps are often clunky compared to big banks. Fewer ATMs. Less robust parental controls.

Best for: Families who value local relationships, fee-free banking, and personalized service over app bells and whistles.

How to Decide

Ask yourself:

  • Do I already bank somewhere? If yes, check if they offer kids' checking accounts. Convenience is underrated.
  • How hands-on do I want to be? If very, consider Chase or Greenlight. If not, Capital One or a credit union works.
  • Is my kid interested in investing? If yes (and they're 13+), Fidelity is worth it.
  • Do I want to pay for extra features? If you're fine with a subscription for robust controls and chore automation, Greenlight is excellent. If not, stick with free options.

There's no wrong answer here. Every account I've listed is fee-free (except Greenlight), safe, and designed for kids. Pick the one that fits your family's style and move forward.

How to Actually Open It

Once you've picked an account, here's the step-by-step process to get it open. I'm giving you the real logistics because "just open an account" is not helpful when you're staring at a website wondering what documents you need.

Step 1: Gather Your Documents

You'll need:

  • Your Social Security number (you're the joint account holder)
  • Your kid's Social Security number
  • Proof of your identity (driver's license or passport)
  • Proof of your address (utility bill, lease agreement, or bank statement — some banks auto-verify this if you already have an account with them)
  • Your kid's date of birth

Some banks also want proof of your kid's identity (birth certificate, passport, or school ID), but many waive this for minors under 13.

Step 2: Decide If You're Opening Online or In-Person

Online: Faster, easier, no appointment needed. Most banks let you open kids' accounts fully digitally now. You'll upload documents (or they'll verify you electronically) and get approved in 5-10 minutes.

In-person: Better if your kid is nervous or excited and wants to "see" the bank. Also good if you have questions or want a banker to explain the account to your kid directly. Call ahead to make sure the branch offers kids' accounts — not all do.

I've done both. Online is faster. In-person feels more official to the kid. Pick based on your kid's personality.

Step 3: Open the Account

If you're doing this online:

  1. Go to the bank's website and find the kids' checking account option
  2. Click "Open Account" and follow the prompts
  3. Enter your information first (you're the primary account holder)
  4. Enter your kid's information
  5. Upload or verify documents
  6. Fund the account (most banks require an initial deposit of $10-$25 — you can transfer this from your existing checking account)
  7. Wait for approval (usually instant, sometimes 1-2 business days)

If you're doing this in person, bring everything I listed in Step 1, show up at the branch with your kid, and let the banker walk you through it.

Step 4: Set Up Parental Controls

This is the step people skip and then regret. Do this immediately after the account is open.

Log into the parent portal or app and configure:

  • Spending limits (daily or weekly caps on how much your kid can spend)
  • Merchant restrictions (block certain stores or categories if you want)
  • Alerts (get a text or email every time your kid makes a purchase — I highly recommend this for the first 6 months)
  • ATM access (decide if your kid can withdraw cash without asking you first)

Every bank's dashboard looks different, but the controls are usually under "Account Settings" or "Parental Controls." Spend 10 minutes here. It's worth it.

Step 5: Wait for the Debit Card (and Explain How to Use It)

The debit card usually arrives in 7-10 business days. When it shows up, sit down with your kid and explain:

  • This card is connected to your account. Every time you use it, money comes out of your account. If you have $50 in your account and spend $10, you'll have $40 left.
  • You need to know your balance before you spend. Show them how to check it (in the app, online, or by calling the bank).
  • If you don't have enough money, the card won't work. Most kids' accounts block overdrafts, which means the transaction just gets declined. Explain that this is embarrassing but not the end of the world.
  • Keep your card safe. If you lose it, we'll cancel it and get a new one, but it's a hassle. Treat it like you'd treat $100 in cash.

Step 6: Make the First Transaction Together

Do not let your kid's first transaction be unsupervised. I don't care if they're 15 and have had a job for a year. The first time they use the card, you're there.

Go to a low-stakes place (a convenience store, a coffee shop, a bookstore) and let them buy something small. Walk them through:

  1. Checking their balance before they go in
  2. Choosing something within their budget
  3. Swiping or tapping the card at checkout
  4. Checking their balance again after the purchase to see the money deducted

This is the moment the abstract becomes real. They see the money disappear from their account. They feel what it's like to spend with a card instead of cash. It's a bigger deal than you think.

What Parents Are Actually Asking

I spend a lot of time reading what parents are genuinely confused about. Here's what's coming up again and again:

"What is the best checking account for a child? My daughter is 8 and I want to start teaching her about money management."

— via Reddit r/FinancialPlanning

At 8, you want maximum parental control and simplicity. I'd go with Chase First Banking if you already bank with Chase, or Capital One MONEY Teen if you don't. Both are free, both have solid parental controls, and both will give her a real debit card she can use. Start there — you can always switch later if it's not working.

"We want to open a bank account for our 7-year-old but I'm not sure if they're ready or which bank to use."

— via Reddit r/Banking

Seven is on the younger end, but it's absolutely doable. Chase First Banking accepts kids as young as 6, and the parental controls are tight enough that you're really just giving your kid a supervised spending tool. The key is to keep the balance low (maybe $20-$30 at a time) and check in weekly. At 7, the goal isn't independence — it's familiarity with how cards and accounts work.

Frequently Asked Questions

What age should a kid get a checking account?

Most kids are ready between ages 8-10 if they can handle basic math, understand that spending means money leaves the account, and have a regular source of income (allowance, birthday money, or earnings). Some banks allow accounts as young as 6 with heavy parental oversight. There's no universal right age — it depends on your kid's maturity and your comfort level.

Can a minor open a checking account without a parent?

No. In the United States, minors cannot legally open bank accounts independently. A parent or guardian must be a joint account holder. This is a legal requirement, not just a bank policy — it protects both the bank and your kid from liability issues like overdrafts or fraud.

What documents do I need to open a checking account for my kid?

You'll typically need: your Social Security number, your kid's Social Security number, your government-issued ID (driver's license or passport), proof of your address, and your kid's date of birth. Some banks also request your kid's birth certificate or school ID, but many waive this for younger children.

Is a checking account or savings account better for kids?

Both serve different purposes. A savings account is better for money your kid is holding long-term (birthday money, saving for something big). A checking account is better for money your kid is actively spending (allowance, earnings from chores or a job). Ideally, your kid has both — and learns to move money between them intentionally.

What's the difference between a kids' checking account and Greenlight?

A kids' checking account (like Capital One MONEY or Chase First Banking) is a real bank account with FDIC insurance, no monthly fee, and a debit card. Greenlight is a prepaid debit card service with a monthly subscription fee ($5.99-$14.98/month) that offers more robust parental controls and features like chore automation and investing. Both are legitimate options — it depends on whether you want to pay for extra features.

Can my kid overdraft their checking account?

Most kids' checking accounts are designed to prevent overdrafts — the transaction simply gets declined if there's not enough money. This is intentional and actually a great teaching moment. Your kid learns that you can't spend money you don't have, without the financial consequences of overdraft fees.
