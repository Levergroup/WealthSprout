How To Teach Kids About Investing — A Parent's Simple Guide

Maya Hartwell Parent-Tested ✓

Former math teacher · Mom of two · Editor, WealthSprout

📅 September 22, 2026 · 7 min read

Most parents wait too long to talk about investing with their kids — and most kids arrive at 18 with no idea what a stock is, how compound interest works, or why starting at 15 instead of 25 makes a $45,000 difference. This guide fixes that. No finance degree required.

Start With Compound Interest — The One Concept That Changes Everything

Before you open any account or explain what a stock is, teach compound interest. It is the only concept in personal finance where the math alone is enough to change a young person's behavior. When kids see the numbers, they get it immediately.

Here is the clearest way to explain it. Start with $100. At a 9% average annual return — roughly what the US stock market has returned historically — that $100 becomes:

  • $260 after 10 years
  • $1,745 after 30 years
  • $11,739 after 50 years

Your child did nothing. They just waited. The money grew on top of itself, over and over, until the original $100 turned into nearly $12,000. That is compound interest. And the earlier you start, the more absurd the result.

The age difference is where the lesson really lands. A 15-year-old who invests $1,000 once and never adds another dollar will have roughly $88,000 at age 65 (at 9% average annual return). The same $1,000 invested at age 25 produces about $43,000. Same money. Same return. The only difference is 10 years of compound growth at the start.

Use a compound interest calculator with your child — let them type in numbers and watch the output change. That five-minute exercise is more effective than any lecture. Try the full compound interest explainer for kids for a worksheet version they can work through on their own.

"The best time to start investing was 10 years ago. The second best time is today. That applies to a 9-year-old just as much as it applies to you."

How To Explain Stocks to Kids Ages 9–12

A stock is a small piece of a company. That is the whole explanation. When you buy a share of Apple, you own a tiny fraction of Apple — the company that made the phone in your pocket. If Apple earns more money and becomes more valuable, your piece becomes worth more too.

The best first exercise is what financial educators call a paper portfolio. It costs nothing and teaches everything:

  1. Ask your child to name three companies they use or care about — the brand that makes their sneakers, the game studio behind their favorite game, the company whose products are in your kitchen.
  2. Look up the stock price together. Most are findable in two seconds on any search engine.
  3. Write down the price and the date. Check it again in a week, a month, three months.
  4. Talk about why the price might have moved — did the company release a new product? Did something happen in the news?

No money is involved. No account is needed. But your child is now thinking like an investor — connecting a company's real-world performance to its stock price.

💡
The Paper Portfolio RuleRun a paper portfolio for 60–90 days before opening a real account. The point is not to practice picking stocks — it is to get comfortable with the vocabulary, the rhythm of checking prices, and the feeling that these numbers connect to real companies doing real things. Boredom is actually good here. Boring means they are learning patience.

Once the paper portfolio runs for a couple of months and your child can explain why a stock went up or down, they are ready to think about real investing. But the first investment for most kids should not be individual stocks — it should be an index fund. Individual stocks are volatile. An index fund owns a piece of every major company in the market, which means your child is not betting on one company — they are betting on the economy as a whole.

What a 13 to 15 Year Old Can Actually Do Right Now

Once your child is in their early teens and has some savings — birthday money, earnings from chores, or money from a first part-time job — they can open a real investment account. This is where the compound interest lesson stops being a math exercise and starts being real life.

For teenagers under 18, the account is called a custodial brokerage account. A parent or guardian opens it and legally owns it until the child reaches adulthood, but the teenager makes the investment decisions. Fidelity Youth Account is one of the strongest options for ages 13–17 because teens can open and manage it themselves with a parent's oversight — no need to hand everything off to a parent.

The first investment should almost always be a broad market index fund. Two strong options with near-zero fees:

  • VTI — Vanguard Total Stock Market ETF. Owns a piece of nearly every publicly traded company in the US. No minimum beyond the share price.
  • FZROX — Fidelity Zero Total Market Index Fund. Identical concept, $0 expense ratio, $0 minimum. Only available at Fidelity.

For teenagers who have earned income — a job, babysitting, lawn care — a Roth IRA is even more powerful. Contributions are limited to earned income (up to $7,000 per year for 2026), grow completely tax-free, and can be withdrawn tax-free in retirement. A 15-year-old contributing $1,000 of summer job earnings to a Roth IRA gets 50+ years of tax-free growth. It is one of the best financial moves a teenager can make. See the full Roth IRA guide for teenagers for the step-by-step account setup process.

Start small. The dollar amount matters far less than the habit. A 14-year-old who invests $25 a month and watches their account grow has already internalized something most adults never learn: that investing is not complicated, not risky in the long run, and not something to wait on.

Frequently Asked Questions

What age should kids start learning about investing?

Most children are ready to understand basic investing concepts around age 9 or 10 — once they can grasp that money can grow over time. Compound interest is the best starting concept. You do not need to open an actual account right away. Starting with the math and a paper portfolio builds genuine understanding before real money enters the picture.

How do I explain stocks to a 10-year-old?

Tell them that a stock is a small piece of a company they already know — like owning a tiny fraction of the company that makes their favorite video game or sneakers. When the company does well and earns more money, that tiny piece becomes worth more. Ask them to pick three companies they use every day and look up the stock price. Watching something they chose go up and down teaches more than any textbook.

Can a teenager legally invest money?

Yes, through a custodial brokerage account. A parent or guardian opens the account and owns it legally until the child reaches adulthood (18 or 21, depending on the state), but the child makes the investment decisions. Fidelity Youth Account is a strong option for teens 13–17 because teenagers can open and manage it themselves with parental oversight.

What is the best first investment for a teenager?

A broad market index fund is the best first investment for most teenagers. VTI (Vanguard Total Stock Market ETF) or FZROX (Fidelity Zero Total Market Index Fund) give exposure to thousands of companies with no research required and extremely low fees. They do not require picking individual stocks, which removes most of the complexity and most of the risk.

How much does a teenager need to start investing?

There is no minimum at most modern brokerages for index funds. FZROX at Fidelity has a $0 minimum. A teenager can start with $25, $50, or whatever they have saved. The amount matters far less than starting. A 15-year-old who invests $1,000 today and never adds another dollar will have roughly $88,000 at age 65 at a 9% average annual return — compared to $43,000 if they wait until 25 to start.

What To Do Next

Teaching kids about investing does not require a finance background. It requires one conversation and a willingness to show them real numbers. Here is where to start:

  1. Show them the compound interest math today. Pull up a calculator and work through the $100 example together. Let them type in numbers. That conversation alone will stick.
  2. Run a paper portfolio for 60 days. Pick three companies they already know. Check prices weekly. Talk about what happened and why. No money at risk, all the learning.
  3. Open a custodial account when they are ready. Fidelity Youth Account is free, simple, and teenager-friendly. Start with FZROX or VTI. Even $25 is enough to begin.
