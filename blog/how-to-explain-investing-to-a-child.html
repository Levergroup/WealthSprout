The Day My Daughter Asked Me to Explain the Stock Market

My 9-year-old looked up from her cereal last spring and asked, "Mom, what's a stock?" She'd overheard a news anchor mention the market was "down 400 points" and apparently decided that sounded catastrophic enough to investigate.

I opened my mouth — and paused. I've paid off $31K in debt. I teach parents about money for a living. And I still fumbled for 10 seconds trying to figure out where to even start. That pause told me everything I needed to know about why so many parents avoid this conversation entirely.

Here's the thing: kids aren't asking for a finance degree. They want to understand the world around them. And when we give them a real, honest answer — in language that actually lands — we hand them something the school system almost never will. According to Next Gen Personal Finance, only 25 states require a personal finance course to graduate. Investing? Barely touched. That gap is on us to fill.

Investing Explained in Terms Your Kid Actually Understands

Forget "buying shares of a company." That sentence means nothing to a 10-year-old and honestly not much more to a lot of adults. Start here instead: investing is putting your money to work so it can grow while you sleep.

Here's the analogy I use with my kids. Imagine your friend is starting a lemonade stand. She needs $10 to buy lemons and cups. You give her the $10, and you both agree that every time she makes a profit, you get a small cut. That's basically what happens when you buy stock in a company — you own a tiny piece, and when the company does well, your piece becomes worth more.

Now let's make it real with numbers, because I'm allergic to vague. Say your kid puts $50 a month into an index fund — think of it as a basket of hundreds of companies all at once, like owning a tiny slice of Amazon, Apple, and Nike simultaneously. If that money grows at an average of 8% per year (the rough historical average of the U.S. stock market, per Investopedia), after 10 years they'd have around $9,000 — from just $6,000 invested. That extra $3,000? That's their money making money. It's called compound growth, and it's genuinely one of the most exciting things I've ever learned to explain.

A few core concepts to introduce, in order of simplicity:

  • Stock: A small ownership piece of a real company
  • Index fund: A bundle of many stocks so you're not betting on just one company
  • Return: The money you earn on top of what you put in
  • Risk: The market goes up AND down — what matters is the long run
  • Time: The longer money is invested, the more it grows — this is their biggest advantage over adults

Don't try to cover all five in one conversation. One concept per dinner is plenty.

How to Start the Investing Conversation at Home (Step by Step)

The best time to open a custodial investment account for your kid was yesterday. The second best time is this weekend — and it takes less than 30 minutes.

Here's exactly how to do it:

  • Step 1 — Open a custodial brokerage account. Fidelity, Schwab, and Vanguard all offer custodial accounts (also called UTMA or UGMA accounts). Fidelity's is called the Youth Account for teens, or you can open a standard custodial account for younger kids. No minimums to open at Fidelity or Schwab.
  • Step 2 — Start with $25–$50/month. That's it. You don't need $500 to start. Set up an automatic monthly transfer so the habit is baked in before the motivation fades.
  • Step 3 — Buy a simple index fund. VOO (Vanguard S&P 500 ETF) or VTI (Vanguard Total Stock Market ETF) are the two I'd point any parent to first. They track the broad market, fees are razor-thin, and they require no guesswork about picking individual stocks.
  • Step 4 — Show your kid the account. Log in together. Point to the number. When it grows, celebrate it. When it dips, talk about why that's normal and not a reason to panic.
  • Step 5 — Connect it to their money. If your kid is already earning money — from chores, a small side hustle, or gifts — help them direct even $5 or $10 of it toward the account. Check out our guide on how to make money as a kid if they need ideas for earning first.

For younger kids (under 8), I'd start even simpler. Get the 3-jar system running first so they understand that money has different jobs. Investing is just the third jar's long-game cousin.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau actually recommends starting financial conversations as early as age 3 — so there is no "too soon." There's only "haven't started yet."

What Parents Are Actually Asking

"How are you teaching your young kids or students about investing? I want to start but don't know where to begin." — via Reddit

This is exactly where most parents get stuck — not from lack of caring, but from not knowing the first move. My honest answer: start with a story, not a definition. Tell your kid about a company they already love, explain that people can own tiny pieces of it, and then open an account together that same week so it stops being abstract.

Beginning is the whole battle. Every week you wait is a week of compound growth your kid doesn't get back.

"I'm teaching my kids 10 and 6 about investing in the stock market. Any suggestions for making it fun and age appropriate?" — via Reddit

For your 10-year-old, try the "stock pick" game — let them choose one company they believe in (Disney, Nike, whatever they're obsessed with), look up the real ticker symbol together, and track the price weekly without actually buying. For your 6-year-old, the lemonade stand analogy I mentioned earlier is gold — keep it that simple and you're winning. Age-appropriate just means meeting them where their brain is, not dumbing it down so much it loses meaning.

The Mistakes Parents Make (And I Made Too)

I didn't start a custodial account for my oldest until she was 7. I kept thinking she needed to "understand it better first." That was the wrong call, and I wish someone had told me sooner.

Here are the four mistakes I see most often — including my own:

  • Waiting until they're "ready." There's no magic age. A 5-year-old can grasp "your money grew" even if they can't explain why. Time in the market beats timing the market — and that's true for kids too.
  • Making it too complicated too fast. I once tried explaining P/E ratios to my daughter in the same conversation as compound interest. Her eyes glazed over in 90 seconds flat. One concept, one conversation. That's the rule now.
  • Only talking about the wins. When the market dips — and it will — a lot of parents go quiet to avoid scaring their kids. But that silence teaches panic. Talk about the dip. Explain it. Show them the historical chart that always goes back up. That's the real lesson.
  • Not involving your kid in the actual account. If they never see the number, it's not real to them. Log in together at least once a month. Let them watch the balance. That visual is worth a hundred explanations.

Quick Wins: 3 Things You Can Do This Week

No waiting until you feel "ready enough." Here's your three-move week:

  • Monday: Have the lemonade stand conversation. Over dinner, tell the lemonade stand story — or use their favorite brand. Ask them: "If you could own a tiny piece of Nike, would you?" Let the curiosity open the door. Ten minutes, no account required yet.
  • Wednesday: Open the account. Go to Fidelity.com or Schwab.com, pull up the custodial account application, and get it done. Have your Social Security number and your kid's handy. Set up a $25 or $50 monthly auto-transfer while you're in there.
  • Saturday: Invest together. Once the account is funded, buy your first shares of VOO or VTI with your kid sitting next to you. Show them the confirmation screen. Screenshot it. This is a moment worth marking — because honestly, I teared up a little the first time I did it for my daughter and that's not embarrassing at all.

Frequently Asked Questions

What age should I start teaching my kid about investing?

Earlier than you think. You can introduce the concept of "money growing" as young as 5 or 6 with simple analogies. By 8 or 9, most kids can understand an index fund in plain terms. The custodial account can be opened the day they're born — you don't have to wait for them to understand it to start it.

Do I need a lot of money to open an account for my kid?

Nope. Fidelity and Schwab have zero minimums on custodial accounts. You can start with $25. The amount matters way less than the habit of starting and staying consistent.

What's the difference between a custodial account and a Roth IRA for kids?

A custodial account (UTMA/UGMA) has no restrictions on what the money is used for when your kid takes control — college, a car, whatever. A Custodial Roth IRA is specifically for retirement and requires your kid to have earned income (like a real job or documented self-employment). Both are great tools — they just have different jobs.

Should I let my kid pick individual stocks?

For educational purposes, sure — it's fun and they'll learn fast. But for their actual account, I'd stick with index funds like VOO or VTI. Picking individual stocks is basically gambling at the level most adults operate at. Teaching your kid to love index funds early is one of the best financial gifts you can give them.

What if the market crashes and my kid gets scared?

Talk about it before it happens. Show them a 50-year chart of the S&P 500 — every crash looks like a blip from far enough out. Then when the market does drop (it will), you can say "remember that chart?" instead of scrambling to explain it in a panic. Framing it as normal is everything.

My kid isn't interested in money at all. How do I get them engaged?

Find the brand they care about and start there. If they're obsessed with Roblox, look up Take-Two Interactive. If they live in Lululemon, pull up LULU. The company becoming "theirs" changes everything — suddenly the stock market isn't abstract, it's personal. That's the hook.
