What To Do With Your First Paycheck as a Teenager — Step by Step

Maya Hartwell Parent-Tested ✓

Former math teacher · Mom of two · Editor, WealthSprout

📅 September 22, 2026 · 6 min read

Your first paycheck looks different from what you expected. The number is smaller. There are lines you do not recognize. And nobody explained any of it. This guide explains everything — what was taken out, why, and exactly what to do with the money that is left.

Understanding Your First Pay Stub — Line by Line

The number on your pay stub labeled gross pay is what you earned. The number labeled net pay is what you actually received. The difference is taxes. Here is what each line means:

  • Federal Income Tax (10–12%): Withheld based on what you put on your W-4 form. Most teenagers are in the 10–12% bracket. If your total earnings this year are under roughly $14,600 (the 2026 standard deduction), you may owe zero federal income tax — and can claim a refund in the spring.
  • Social Security (6.2%): Goes to the Social Security fund. Always withheld. Cannot be avoided.
  • Medicare (1.45%): Goes to Medicare. Always withheld. Cannot be avoided.
  • State Income Tax (varies): Depends on your state. Some states have no income tax. Others range from 2–5% for low earners.

The W-4 form you filled out on your first day tells your employer how much federal tax to withhold. If you expect to earn less than the standard deduction this year, you can write "Exempt" on line 4c of the W-4, which stops federal withholding entirely. Social Security and Medicare still come out regardless. If you are not sure, ask your employer or a parent before your next paycheck.

The Exact Split to Make With Every Paycheck

Most teenagers spend everything within a week of getting paid. This is not a character flaw — it is what happens when there is no system. The split is the system. Apply it to every paycheck, automatically, before you spend anything.

Here is the split that works:

  • 10% Give — Generosity is a habit, not a personality. Ten percent of every paycheck keeps it automatic.
  • 30% Save — Short-term savings for a specific goal: a car, a trip, a piece of equipment, a few months of emergency cushion. Use a separate savings account.
  • 20% Invest — Long-term, hands-off, index funds. This money is not for spending. It is for your 30-year-old self and your 60-year-old self. More on this in the next section.
  • 40% Spend — Everything left is yours to spend without guilt. Gas, food, entertainment, whatever you want.
$1,000,000+
What $200/month invested starting at age 16 becomes by age 60, at a 7% average annual return. The same $200/month starting at age 26 produces roughly $510,000 — the 10-year head start is worth over $490,000.

This split is more aggressive than most advice you will see. That is intentional. You are in the only period of your life where you have income, minimal expenses, and time on your side simultaneously. This combination never returns. Teenagers who build this habit find that it follows them into adulthood — when expenses are real, the system is already automatic.

"The split you set on your first paycheck is the split you will default to for the rest of your working life. Set it high now, while the habits are forming."

Opening Your First Investment Account as a Teenager

The 20% invest portion of every paycheck needs a real account — not a savings account, not cash under the mattress. A brokerage account.

If you have earned income from a job — wages, tips, babysitting, lawn care — you qualify to open a Roth IRA. This is the single best account a teenager can use. Contributions grow completely tax-free. Withdrawals in retirement are tax-free. And because you are starting decades before most people, the compound growth is extraordinary. See the full Roth IRA guide for teenagers for the step-by-step setup process.

If you do not have earned income yet, a custodial brokerage account works well. A parent opens it, you manage it. Fidelity Youth Account is the strongest option for ages 13–17 — teenagers can manage it independently while a parent maintains oversight. No minimum balance, no fees, straightforward interface.

Once the account is open, your first investment should almost always be an index fund — not individual stocks. Two strong options:

  • FZROX — Fidelity Zero Total Market Index Fund. $0 minimum, $0 expense ratio. Owns a slice of the entire US stock market. Available only at Fidelity.
  • VTI — Vanguard Total Stock Market ETF. Same concept, available at any brokerage. Low expense ratio.

You do not need to research companies. You do not need to watch the market daily. Set up automatic transfers from your paycheck — even $25 or $50 per paycheck — and let compound interest do the work. For the math behind why starting now matters, read the compound interest guide.

The Three Mistakes Most Teenagers Make With Their First Paycheck

These are the three patterns that reliably set teenagers back — not from bad intentions, but from not knowing what the right move looks like.

Mistake 1: Spending it all before any system is in place

The default is to spend. Without a split already decided, everything goes. Set the split before your first paycheck arrives. Transfer money to savings and investing accounts the same day the paycheck hits. What stays in checking is what you spend. Full stop.

Mistake 2: Saving everything and investing nothing

Saving is good. But money sitting in a savings account earning 4–5% is not the same as money in an index fund compounding at a historical average of 7–10%. Savings is for goals 0–3 years away. Investing is for goals 10+ years away. Both matter. Neither replaces the other.

Mistake 3: Waiting until you "earn more" to invest

The amount matters far less than the timing. A 16-year-old who invests $50 a month for two years and then stops entirely will outperform a 26-year-old investing $200 a month — because the first 10 years of compound growth are the most powerful. There is no threshold to cross before starting. The threshold is now.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my first paycheck look smaller than I expected?

Because taxes are withheld before you see the money. Federal income tax, Social Security (6.2%), and Medicare (1.45%) come out of every paycheck automatically. Most teenagers are in the 10–12% federal tax bracket. If you earned less than roughly $14,600 this year, you may get most of the withheld federal income tax back as a refund when you file your return in the spring.

What percentage of my paycheck should I save as a teenager?

A strong starting split is 10% Give, 30% Save, 20% Invest, 40% Spend. This is more aggressive on the savings side than most people suggest — but teenagers have no fixed expenses, which means this is the easiest time in your life to save a high percentage. The habit you build now carries forward into adulthood when expenses are harder to control.

Can a 16-year-old open a bank account?

Yes. Most banks offer accounts for teens 13 and older, typically requiring a parent or guardian to co-sign. Some accounts like Fidelity Youth Account can be opened at 13 and give teenagers more independence while maintaining parental oversight. Having your own account before your first paycheck is the right move — do not let your earnings sit in cash.

Should I invest my first paycheck as a teenager?

Yes — even a small amount. A teenager who invests $200 a month starting at age 16 will have over $1 million by age 60 at a 7% average annual return. Starting at 26 with the same $200 a month produces roughly $510,000. The 10-year head start is worth more than $490,000 — just from starting early.

What is a W-4 form and how should a teenager fill it out?

A W-4 tells your employer how much federal income tax to withhold from your paycheck. As a teenager who earns less than the standard deduction (roughly $14,600 in 2026), you may qualify to claim "exempt" from federal withholding — meaning no federal income tax is withheld. You still owe Social Security and Medicare. Ask your employer or a parent to help you fill it out correctly the first time.

What To Do Right Now

Before your next paycheck lands, do three things:

  1. Set your split. Decide the four percentages — Give, Save, Invest, Spend — and write them down. 10/30/20/40 is a proven starting point. Adjust from there.
  2. Open your investing account. Fidelity Youth Account if you are 13–17. Roth IRA if you have earned income. FZROX or VTI as your first investment.
  3. Automate the transfers. Set up automatic transfers on payday. The money that moves automatically is the money you actually save and invest. Manual transfers get skipped.

The first paycheck is not just income. It is the moment your financial life begins. What you do with it right now sets a pattern that either compounds in your favor for decades — or does not. You are here, reading this. That puts you far ahead. Go open the account.
