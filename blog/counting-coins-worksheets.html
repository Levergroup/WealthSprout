Only 1 in 3 Fourth Graders Is Proficient in Math, and Coins Are Part of the Story

On the 2022 NAEP, the national math assessment often called the Nation's Report Card, only about 36% of fourth graders scored at or above proficient. That's roughly 1 in 3, and it was a drop from 2019. Coin counting is one of the first places kids hit real-world math, so it's a spot where the gaps show up early.

Here's the twist that surprised me. Your kid probably sees fewer coins than you did. Federal Reserve payment surveys put cash at around 1 in 6 purchases now, which means a lot of kids grow up tapping a card or phone and never handling a quarter.

I taught middle school math for years, and I watched kids who could do fractions stall on "how much is three dimes and a nickel?" It wasn't a smarts problem. They'd just never held the stuff.

So when a coin worksheet comes home in the backpack, it's a fine tool. It just can't be the only one, and it works much better once you know what it's missing.

📌 Key Takeaways

  • Kids learn coins best by handling real ones first, then using worksheets for practice.
  • The dime is the trickiest coin: it's the smallest but worth 10¢, more than the nickel or penny.
  • Skip counting by 5s, 10s, and 25s is the real skill behind counting coins.
  • Ten minutes of hands-on play beats thirty minutes of worksheet grinding.
  • Coin skills stick when they connect to something your kid actually wants to buy or save for.

The Real Issue: Why Worksheets Alone Don't Work

A worksheet can make a kid look like they understand coins while they're only matching pictures to numbers. I learned this the hard way with my 9-year-old when she was in first grade. She breezed through a page of "circle the 25¢ coin" and then couldn't pick a quarter out of my purse.

Worksheets show a flat drawing of a coin, usually in black and white. Real coins have weight, texture, color, and size, and those are the clues kids actually use. A drawn dime and a drawn nickel look almost identical on paper, but in your hand they feel completely different.

The second problem is that a page full of "count the coins" problems tests addition, not coin knowledge. If your kid can't skip count by 5s yet, a worksheet with six nickels will feel like a wall. They'll guess, get it wrong, and decide they're bad at money.

That's a fixable gap, not a talent gap. I didn't see it clearly until I'd watched it happen with my own kid, and then the fix turned out to be simple:your kid's hands before the worksheet ever comes out.

Once she'd sorted a handful of real coins by feel, the same worksheet that stumped her took about four minutes. Nothing about the page changed. She just had something real to hang it on.

So think of worksheets as the last step, not the first. They're great for checking what stuck and for building speed. They're lousy at teaching a coin from scratch.

Step-by-Step: How to Actually Teach Coin Counting

This is the order that worked for us, and it's close to what I used with students back in the classroom. Don't rush it. If your kid stalls on a step, stay there a few days.

Step 1: Let Them Handle the Coins

Dump a mixed handful of pennies, nickels, dimes, and quarters on the table. Ask your kid to sort them any way they want. Don't correct anything yet.

They'll usually sort by color or size, and that's a great start. Then you can say, "Which ones are silver? Which one's copper?" Let them notice the edges too, since the dime and quarter are ridged while the penny and nickel are smooth.

Step 2: Name the Coins and Their Values

Now add names and numbers, one coin at a time. Start with the penny (1¢) and the nickel (5¢), because they're the easiest to separate by color. Then bring in the dime (10¢) and the quarter (25¢).

The dime deserves its own moment. It's the smallest coin but worth more than the nickel, and that trips up almost every kid. Put a dime next to a nickel and say, "Small, but mighty."

Step 3: Practice Skip Counting Out Loud

This is the skill that actually makes coin counting work. Before you add any coins together, make sure your kid can count by 5s, 10s, and 25s. Do it in the car, on walks, or while you're waiting for dinner.

Then line up five nickels and count them together: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25. Touch each coin as you say the number. The touching matters more than you'd think.

Step 4: Count Biggest to Smallest

Mixed piles are where kids get lost. Teach one rule: start with the biggest value and work down. Quarters first, then dimes, then nickels, then pennies.

So for two quarters, a dime, and three pennies, your kid says "25, 50, 60, 61, 62, 63." It sounds almost like a song once it clicks. I'll admit my daughter hated this rule at first and wanted to count in whatever order the coins landed.

Step 5: Bring In the Worksheet

Now the worksheet earns its spot. Your kid has held the coins, said the values, and practiced skip counting, so the page finally has something to connect to. Let them keep a few real coins next to them while they work.

If they get stuck, don't tell them the answer. Say, "Grab the coins and build it." Then check their work on the page afterward.

Step 6: Move to Making Change

Once counting is solid, flip it around. Give your kid a "price" like 35¢ and ask them to pay with real coins in two different ways. This is where coin skill turns into money sense.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's youth financial education resources are a good place to find free activities that build on this once your kid is ready.

What Parents Get Wrong About Coin Worksheets

I've made most of these mistakes myself, so no judgment here. Here's what I see most often, in my own house and with other parents.

Starting with Mixed Coins Too Soon

A worksheet with a quarter, two dimes, a nickel, and four pennies is a lot for a beginner. It asks them to identify, recall values, skip count, and add, all at once. Start with one type of coin per page, then two, then mix them.

Treating Wrong Answers as Carelessness

When my daughter missed a problem, I used to say, "Slow down and look again." That was the wrong move. Usually she wasn't rushing. She was missing a step, like not knowing the dime's value or losing her place while skip counting.

Now I ask her to walk me through how she got the answer. Her mistake usually shows up in the first ten seconds.

Doing Too Much in One Sitting

Coin counting is tiring for a young kid. Twenty problems in a row will leave them frustrated and wanting to quit. Ten minutes is plenty, and five is fine for a 6-year-old.

Skipping the "Why"

Kids put up with practice a lot longer when they know where it leads. Tell your kid, "This is how you'll know if you have enough for that toy." Adding a goal turns the coins from homework into something that belongs to them, and it's the same idea behind our 3-jar system for kids.

Quick Wins: 5 Activities That Work Better Than Worksheets

These take almost no prep. Pick one and try it this week.

1. The Coin Treasure Hunt

Hide 10 to 15 coins around a room and let your kid hunt them down. When they've found them all, they sort and count their haul. Nobody complains about math when there's treasure involved.

2. Pretend Store

Stick prices on a few snacks or toys, from 5¢ to 50¢. Your kid is the shopper, and you're the cashier who only takes exact change. Then swap roles, because counting out change is harder than paying.

3. Coin Rubbings

Put a coin under a sheet of paper and rub the side of a crayon over it. Your kid will see the details on each coin come up, like the faces and the numbers. It's a quiet, no-pressure way to learn what each coin looks like up close.

4. The Piggy Bank Count

Once a week, empty a jar or piggy bank and count it together. Let your kid make the stacks and call out the totals. If you want to take it further, our guide on how to make money as a kid has ideas for earning coins worth counting.

5. Coin Bingo or War

Flip two coins at once and ask which one's worth more, then which two together make more. Or play a version of "War" where each player flips a coin and the higher value wins. It's quick and silly, and it builds value recall faster than you'd expect.

What Parents Are Actually Asking

These are the questions I hear most from other parents, in comments, emails, and the school pickup line.

"My 7-year-old can count to 100 but freezes when I hand her a pile of coins. What's going wrong?"

This is super common, and it's usually not a math problem. Counting by ones is a different skill than counting by 5s, 10s, and 25s. Start with skip counting out loud, then bring in the coins one type at a time.

Give it a few weeks. My daughter went from freezing to showing off in about a month of ten-minute sessions.

"Is it even worth teaching coins when we mostly use cards and our phones?"

I get why you'd ask, and I wondered the same thing. But coins are the easiest way to make value feel real, because your kid can see and hold what "25" looks like. That sense carries over to dollars, budgeting, and later on, to understanding ideas like saving and interest.

Think of coins as training wheels for number sense. Cards and apps come later, and they'll make more sense once your kid knows what money actually is.

Frequently Asked Questions

What age should kids start learning to count coins?

Most kids are ready around ages 5 to 7, usually in kindergarten or first grade. They need to be comfortable counting to 100 and starting to skip count. If your kid's younger and curious, sorting coins by color and size is a fine place to begin.

Why do kids mix up nickels and dimes?

Because size and value don't match up. The nickel is bigger but worth 5¢, and the dime is smaller but worth 10¢. Handling both side by side and repeating "small but mighty" for the dime helps a lot.

How long should a coin counting practice session be?

Aim for 5 to 10 minutes for younger kids and up to 15 for older ones. Short and frequent beats long and rare. Stop while your kid's still feelingconfident instead of frustrated. Ending on a small win means your kid will actually say yes to tomorrow's session.

Should my kid use real coins or just the worksheet?

Both, if you can swing it. Worksheets are great for practice and for seeing how your kid thinks, but real coins let them feel the size and weight differences that pictures can't show.

A handful of coins from the couch cushions works fine. I'll admit I've bribed my own kids with "find-the-quarters" hunts more than once.

My kid keeps mixing up nickels and dimes. Is that normal?

Totally normal. A dime is worth less than a nickel but is physically smaller, and that trips up a lot of kids.

Try sorting real coins by size, then by value, and let your kid say out loud what makes each one different. Skip-counting by 5s and 10s together also helps the values stick, and it's fine if it takes a few weeks.

When should my kid move on to making change?

There's no magic age, and I don't think anyone has a perfect answer. A good sign is when your kid can count a mixed pile of coins to a dollar without much hesitation.

From there, try simple "I have a dollar, the snack costs 65 cents" questions. If it gets messy, step back to counting and try again in a couple of weeks.
