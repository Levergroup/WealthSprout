How to Open a Bank Account for My Son

If you're Googling "how to open a bank account for my son," I'm going to guess one of two things just happened: your son asked you for one, or he's been spending cash so fast lately that you realized he needs one. Either way — you're in the right place, and honestly, this is one of the best money moves you can make for him right now.

I didn't open a real bank account until I was in college. Nobody showed me how. I just... had cash in a drawer and then suddenly had a debit card I had no idea how to use responsibly. I'm not saying that ruined my financial life, but it definitely didn't help the $31K in debt I found myself staring down in my early thirties. One of the core reasons I built WealthSprout is so your kid doesn't have to learn all of this the hard way, the way I did.

So let's walk through this step by step — from figuring out when he's actually ready, to the exact documents you'll need, to which banks are genuinely worth your time.

Age-First: When to Start

Here's my honest take: most kids are ready earlier than parents expect. You don't need to wait until your son is 16 and has a job. In fact, waiting that long means you've missed years of hands-on practice with real money in a real account — which is the only way any of this actually sticks.

Ages 6–8: The Savings Account Starter

At this age, your son can absolutely understand that money lives somewhere safe, that it can grow, and that he can check on it. He won't need a debit card yet. A basic custodial savings account where he watches his birthday money sit (and ideally earn a tiny bit of interest) is perfect. My 9-year-old was obsessed with checking the balance every week after we opened hers. That excitement? That's the lesson.

Ages 9–12: Ready for More Structure

This is the sweet spot for opening a kids' checking account or a platform like Greenlight. Your son can start connecting spending decisions to a real balance. He can see what it looks like when money goes down after a purchase. That's genuinely powerful — and it's something no classroom has ever taught consistently.

Ages 13–17: Teen Checking + Debit Card Time

If your son is in this range and doesn't have a bank account yet, let's fix that today. At this age, he may have income from chores, odd jobs, or even a part-time job — and keeping that in a shoebox or a cash envelope isn't doing him any favors. Teen checking accounts from banks like Step or Capital One MONEY give him real autonomy with guardrails you control.

What Your Son Is Ready For by Age Group

Opening the account is just step one. What you teach him to do with it matters even more. Here's a quick breakdown of realistic expectations:

Ages 6–8

  • Understands that a bank holds money safely
  • Can watch a balance grow over time
  • Can practice the habit of "saving before spending" (the 3-jar system is great here)
  • Not ready for a debit card — and that's fine

Ages 9–12

  • Can use a prepaid debit card with a set weekly or monthly allowance loaded onto it
  • Understands that spending money means less money in the account
  • Can start tracking purchases (some apps make this visual and almost game-like)
  • Ready to talk about the difference between wants and needs — without it being a lecture

Ages 13–17

  • Can manage a real checking account with a debit card
  • Can start earning money independently — check out ways kids can earn money for ideas
  • Ready to understand interest (both savings interest and — importantly — debt interest)
  • Can begin thinking about savings goals with actual timelines

How to Start the Banking Conversation Without Lecturing

This is the part most parenting finance articles skip, and it drives me a little crazy. You can pick the perfect bank, gather all the documents, and still have your son completely checked out if the conversation leading up to it feels like a lecture.

Here's what works: make it about him, not about money lessons.

Try something like: "Hey, I was thinking — you've got some money saved up and I want you to actually be able to use it and track it. Want to open your own bank account? You'd have your own debit card."

For a younger kid, it might be: "You know how we put your birthday money in that envelope? What if it lived in a real bank account, and you could check it on my phone whenever you wanted?"

The magic words are "your own." Kids — especially boys in that 10–14 range — respond really well to ownership and autonomy. Don't lead with "this will teach you responsibility." Lead with "this is yours."

Also: involve him in picking the bank or app. Show him two options. Let him choose. That small act of participation makes him ten times more likely to actually engage with the account once it's open.

For more on teaching saving as a concept before the account conversation, I'd recommend starting with how to teach kids about saving money — it has some solid conversation starters by age.

Account Types Explained Clearly

Okay, this is where things get a little jargon-y in the banking world, and I want to cut through that. Here are the main types of accounts you'll encounter and what they actually mean:

Custodial Savings Account

You (the parent) are the legal owner of the account until your son reaches adulthood — usually 18 or 21 depending on your state. He's a beneficiary, not an account holder. This is common for younger kids. The money is his in practice, but you're in control legally. Good for: ages 6–10, lower balances, no debit card needed yet.

Per Investopedia's overview of custodial accounts, these are straightforward to open and have no contribution limits — which makes them a flexible starting point.

Joint Checking or Savings Account

Both you and your son are account holders. Either of you can make deposits or withdrawals. This is common for teens who are 13+ and have some income. The advantage: real banking experience with a safety net. You can see everything. Good for: ages 13–17.

Teen Checking Account

Many banks now offer checking accounts specifically designed for minors, with parental oversight built in. These often include a debit card, mobile app access, and spending notifications sent to your phone. Think of it as a joint account with extra guardrails. Good for: ages 13–17.

Kids' Prepaid Debit Card Accounts

These aren't traditional bank accounts — they're prepaid card platforms with banking-like features. Greenlight is the big name here. You load money onto the card, set spending rules by category, and get real-time notifications. Good for: ages 8–14 who aren't quite ready for a full checking account.

High-Yield Savings Account (HYSA)

I didn't know these existed until I was 34 — I'm completely serious. A HYSA is a savings account that pays significantly more interest than a standard savings account. As of 2024–2025, many are paying around 4–5% APY versus the 0.01% at big traditional banks. If your son has $500+ saved and won't need it immediately, parking it in an HYSA at Alliant Credit Union or a similar institution is a genuinely smart move. Good for: any age with meaningful savings.

What You'll Need (Exact Document List)

Nothing kills the momentum of "let's open your account today!" like showing up to a bank — or hitting a wall on an app — because you're missing something. Here's exactly what you'll need:

For You (the Parent/Guardian)

  • Government-issued photo ID (driver's license or passport)
  • Your Social Security Number
  • Your current address (some banks verify this with a utility bill or bank statement)
  • Email address and phone number

For Your Son

  • Social Security Number (required for nearly all accounts — yes, even for kids)
  • Date of birth
  • Full legal name (make sure it matches his Social Security card)
  • For some accounts: a school ID, state ID, or passport if he's a teen

To Fund the Account

  • An opening deposit (many accounts require $0–$25 to open; some have no minimum)
  • Your existing bank account info if you're transferring funds digitally, or cash if opening in person

Pro tip: if your son doesn't have a Social Security Number memorized (most kids don't), pull the card from your files before you start the application. You'll thank me later.

Best Banks and Apps for Kids (Specific Picks)

I'm not going to give you a vague "shop around for the best rates" answer here. Here are real options with real reasons to consider each one:

Chase First Banking (Ages 6–17)

No monthly fee if you have a Chase account. Your son gets a debit card, you get spending controls and real-time alerts. There are no overdrafts possible, which is great for younger kids. The app is solid. One limitation: you need to be an existing Chase customer. Best for: families already banking with Chase who want a seamless setup.

Capital One MONEY (Ages 8+)

Free to open, no monthly fees, no minimum balance. Both you and your son get app access, but he has his own login — which he'll love. It also earns a small amount of interest (more than most traditional kids' accounts). Best for: parents who want a low-friction, no-cost option with decent features.

Greenlight (Ages 5–18)

This is a paid platform ($5.99–$14.98/month depending on the plan), but it's genuinely feature-rich. You can set spending rules by store category, automate allowance, assign chores, and even invest in stocks through the app together. My personal pick for ages 8–13 who need training wheels on their spending. Best for: parents who want maximum control and are willing to pay a small monthly fee for it.

Step (Ages 13–18)

Step is interesting because it functions like a checking account but also helps teens start building credit history — without the risk of actual credit card debt. It's free, has a Visa card, and the app is genuinely teen-friendly. Best for: teens 13+ who want to feel like they have a "real" account.

Alliant Credit Union (Teens + Parents Who Want an HYSA)

If you want your son's savings to actually earn meaningful interest, Alliant consistently offers competitive HYSA rates. They have teen checking accounts too. There's a $5 membership "donation" to open, but no ongoing fees. Best for: families focused on saving rather than spending features.

Copper Banking (Ages 13–17)

Copper is newer and geared specifically toward financial education for teens. The app teaches budgeting concepts alongside the actual banking features. It's free. Best for: teens who are curious about money and want to learn, not just spend.

For additional comparison and unbiased context on these products, the CFPB's consumer banking tools are worth bookmarking.

What Parents Are Actually Asking (From Reddit + Quora)

I spent some time in the Reddit trenches so you don't have to. Here's what real parents are actually asking — and my honest answers:

From r/Banking: "I set up my kids' banking way in advance — like, before they even asked for it. Is that weird or is that smart planning?"

Maya's take: That's genuinely smart, and I wish more parents thought this way. Opening a savings account when your son is 6 or 7 — even with just $50 — means he grows up knowing it exists. It becomes normal. When he's 13 and wants to talk about money, you've already built the foundation. Not weird at all. Proactive.

From r/personalfinance: "My son is 14 and wants his own bank account. Do I have to be on it? I want him to feel like it's actually his."

Maya's take: Yes, legally you have to be on it until he's 18 (in most states). But here's the thing — "being on it" doesn't mean hovering over every transaction. You can give him full day-to-day control while still having visibility. Accounts like Step and Capital One MONEY let him have his own login and his own experience. The legal joint ownership is just a formality at that point. Tell him that. It helps.

From r/Banking: "Someone mentioned Chase First Banking is designed for children ages 6–17. Has anyone actually used it? Is it worth it if we already bank with Chase?"

Maya's take: Yes, it's worth it if you're already a Chase customer — specifically because of the zero extra fees and the seamless integration with your existing account. The parental controls are genuinely useful (you can block certain merchant categories), and the setup takes about 10 minutes online. The main downside is that the educational features are pretty minimal compared to something like Greenlight. It's a bank account, not a financial education platform. If you want both, pair it with free resources from Next Gen Personal Finance — they have excellent, free teen-friendly money lessons.

FAQ: Opening a Bank Account for Your Son

Can I open a bank account for my son without him being present?

For online applications, yes — you can often complete the entire process yourself using his information. For in-branch openings, many banks prefer (or require) that the minor be present, especially for teens. Check with your specific bank before you drive over. For younger kids (under 10), most banks are fine with parent-only visits.

What's the minimum age to open a bank account for my son?

There's no legal minimum age — it depends on the bank. Many banks and apps accept accounts for children as young as 6 (Chase First Banking explicitly lists 6–17). Some platforms like Greenlight go down to age 5. The real question isn't the minimum age; it's whether your son is old enough to understand that the money in the account is real and finite.

Do I need my son's Social Security Number to open the account?

Yes, in virtually all cases. Banks are required to collect SSNs for all account holders under federal law (specifically the USA PATRIOT Act). This applies to minors too. If you've misplaced his Social Security card, you can request a replacement through the Social Security Administration before you open the account.

What happens to the account when my son turns 18?

This depends on the account type. For custodial accounts, the account legally transfers to your son at the age of majority (18 in most states, 21 in a few). For joint accounts, you'll typically convert the account to a standard individual account in his name alone — the bank will usually notify you both when this is coming. Some teen-specific accounts (like Step) transition automatically to adult accounts.

Is there a way to open an account with no fees?

Yes — several. Capital One MONEY is completely free. Chase First Banking is free if you're an existing Chase customer. Copper is free. Step is free. Where you'll run into fees is with Greenlight (which charges a monthly subscription) or if you open a savings account at a traditional bank with a minimum balance requirement you don't meet. Always read the fee schedule before opening anything.

My son has a job and wants to set up direct deposit. Can he do that as a minor?

Yes! This is actually one of the most exciting financial milestones, and a teen checking account handles it perfectly. Step and Capital One MONEY both support direct deposit. Your son's employer will just need the routing and account numbers from his account — same process as any adult. The fact that it's a joint account with you on it doesn't affect direct deposit at all.

Opening a bank account for your son doesn't have to be complicated, and it doesn't have to be a big formal event. It can literally be a Tuesday afternoon after school — "hey, want to set this up together?" — and 20 minutes later he has an account, a debit card on the way, and a real reason to start thinking about money differently. That's the goal. Small step, big shift.

Start with the account type that matches his age, grab the documents from the list above, and pick one of the specific banks or apps I mentioned. You've got this — and so does he.
