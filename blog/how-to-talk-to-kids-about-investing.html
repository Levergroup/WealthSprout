How to Talk to Kids About Investing (Without Their Eyes Glazing Over)

Former math teacher · Mom of two · Editor, WealthSprout

Here's something that kept me up at night when my oldest turned 14: she could recite the quadratic formula, name every element on the periodic table, and write a five-paragraph essay about the symbolism in To Kill a Mockingbird. But when I asked her what a stock was, she said, "Isn't that, like, soup base?" And honestly? That's not her fault. It's ours. We're raising kids in a system that teaches them about money (here's how to make change!) but never teaches them how to grow it.

I didn't understand investing until I was 34 years old. Thirty-four. I'd been a teacher, paid bills, even managed a household budget — but the stock market felt like this mysterious club for people in suits. When I finally figured out that investing isn't about picking hot stocks or timing the market, but about putting small amounts into boring index funds and waiting decades, I was genuinely mad. Why didn't anyone tell me sooner? That's exactly why I built WealthSprout: so your kid doesn't have to wait until their mid-thirties to figure this out. Let's talk about how to have this conversation — and I promise, it's way less awkward than you think.

The Problem: Why Most Kids Never Learn to Invest

Let's be real: most of us didn't learn about investing from our parents because our parents didn't know either. It's this weird generational game of telephone where nobody actually learned the rules. My mom's financial advice was "save your money and don't touch it," which is fine, but she kept her savings in a checking account earning 0.01% interest while inflation ate it alive. She wasn't wrong — she just didn't have the information.

The American school system doesn't help. As of 2024, only about half of states require any personal finance education for high school graduation, and even then, "investing" is usually one chapter that gets skipped when time runs short. According to the National Endowment for Financial Education (NGPF), most high school seniors can't explain the difference between a stock and a bond. Meanwhile, we expect them to make major financial decisions — student loans, credit cards, retirement accounts — approximately ten minutes after they graduate.

The other problem? Investing has a marketing issue. Pop culture shows us Wolf of Wall Street types screaming into phones, or crypto bros promising lamborghinis. It looks complicated, risky, and honestly kind of sleazy. No wonder teens think investing is either gambling or something only rich people do. The reality — that boring, consistent investing in diversified funds is how regular people actually build wealth — doesn't make for good Netflix content.

The Numbers That Should Scare You (But Won't After This)

Okay, here's the stat that physically hurt me when I first saw it: if your 15-year-old invests just $100 per month starting now and earns an average 10% annual return (the historical average for the S&P 500), they'll have over $1.1 million by age 65. If they wait until 35 to start? They'll have about $226,000. Same monthly contribution. Same return rate. Nearly $900,000 difference — all because of time. This is compound interest, and it's the closest thing to financial magic that exists.

Here's another one: a study from the SEC found that 66% of Americans couldn't correctly answer basic questions about compound interest. Two-thirds of us don't understand the most powerful wealth-building concept in existence. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) reports that young adults who received financial education were significantly more likely to have savings accounts and less likely to be in debt. The research is clear: early financial education changes outcomes.

But here's the number that actually gives me hope: your teen doesn't need to invest $1,000 a month to see meaningful results. Even $25 per month — roughly one less fast food trip per week — adds up. The barrier to entry has never been lower. Most brokerages have no minimums now. Your kid can literally invest their birthday money and start building wealth today.

The Solution: How to Actually Start the Conversation

Forget sitting your teen down for a "very important talk" about investing. That approach works exactly never. (If your teen is anything like mine, their eyes will glaze over faster than you can say "diversified portfolio.") Instead, you're going to do something sneakier: you're going to make it relevant to something they already care about.

My go-to conversation starter is this: "Hey, you know how you love Nike shoes? What if every time someone bought Nikes, you got a tiny piece of that sale?" That's stock ownership in one sentence. When you own shares of a company, you own a tiny piece of that business. When Apple sells an iPhone, shareholders get a cut. When McDonald's sells a burger, shareholders benefit. Suddenly, investing isn't some abstract Wall Street thing — it's your kid owning a piece of brands they actually recognize.

The next step is showing them what their own money could do. Open up a compound interest calculator (there are free ones everywhere) and let them plug in their own numbers. If they've been making money through side gigs or part-time jobs, ask them: "What if instead of that money just sitting in your account, it could grow?" Teens respond way better to "here's what YOUR money could do" than "here's what you SHOULD do."

How to Talk to Your Kid About Investing (Step by Step)

Step 1: Start with the "why" before the "how." Before you explain ETFs and index funds, your teen needs to understand why this matters. I like to use the "working money vs. waiting money" framing. Money sitting in a bank account is waiting money — it's just hanging out, maybe earning 0.01% interest, slowly losing value to inflation. Investing puts your money to work, so it earns more money while you're sleeping, going to school, or binge-watching whatever's popular this week. The goal is to have your money work as hard as you do.

Step 2: Explain the market without overcomplicating it. The stock market is basically a giant store where people buy and sell tiny pieces of companies. When lots of people want to buy a piece of Apple, the price goes up. When people want to sell, it goes down. Over short periods, prices bounce around like crazy. Over long periods (we're talking decades), the market has historically gone up about 10% per year on average. That's why investing is a long game — you're not trying to get rich next week, you're building wealth over 10, 20, 40 years.

Step 3: Introduce one specific investment: VOO. Don't overwhelm them with options. Start with one index fund: VOO (Vanguard S&P 500 ETF). When you buy VOO, you're buying a tiny piece of the 500 largest companies in America — Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, Google, all of them — in one purchase. It's like buying a sampler platter instead of ordering 500 individual items. The expense ratio (the fee you pay) is only 0.03% — that's $3 per year for every $10,000 invested. It's boring, it's not sexy, and it's exactly what actual financial advisors recommend for long-term wealth building.

Step 4: Open a custodial account or Roth IRA together. If your teen has earned income from a job, babysitting, or any legitimate side hustle, they might be eligible for a Roth IRA. This is a retirement account where you invest after-tax money, and all the growth is completely tax-free forever. If they put in $1,000 at 16 and it grows to $50,000 by retirement, they owe zero taxes on that $49,000 gain. If they don't have earned income, you can open a custodial brokerage account (like Fidelity Youth Account or Schwab Custodial) where you manage it until they're 18, then it becomes theirs.

Step 5: Set up automatic investing and then leave it alone. The real secret to successful investing isn't picking the right stocks — it's consistency. Help your teen set up automatic monthly investments, even if it's just $25 or $50. If they're using the three-jar system, this could come from their "save" or "invest" jar. The goal is to make investing invisible so they're not tempted to time the market or panic when prices dip. My rule with my own kids: we check our investments once a quarter, max. No obsessing over daily fluctuations.

What School Doesn't Teach About Investing

School teaches you that Columbus discovered America, the mitochondria is the powerhouse of the cell, and you definitely need to know how to calculate the area of a trapezoid. What school doesn't teach is that if your teen invests $3,000 per year in a Roth IRA starting at age 16, they could have over $2.5 million by retirement — and never pay a dime in taxes on those gains. The opportunity cost of not learning this early is genuinely heartbreaking.

Here's what else gets left out: the difference between investing and speculating. Buying an S&P 500 index fund and holding it for 30 years is investing. Buying a meme stock because someone on TikTok said "to the moon" is speculating (or gambling, depending on how generous you're feeling). Schools also don't teach that market "crashes" are normal — they happen roughly every 7-10 years — and that every single time in history, the market has eventually recovered and gone higher. The kids who understand this won't panic-sell during the next downturn. They'll keep investing and buy stocks "on sale."

The biggest thing school doesn't teach? That you don't need to be rich to invest. You don't need to understand P/E ratios or read financial statements. You don't need a finance degree. You need time, consistency, and one boring index fund. That's it. The financial industry wants you to think investing is complicated so you'll pay them to manage your money. The truth is, a 15-year-old with a Roth IRA and automatic monthly investments into VOO is doing exactly what most financial advisors would recommend anyway.

What Parents Are Actually Asking

When I started talking about this topic online, I found parents asking the same questions everywhere. Here are a few real concerns from forums that I think you might relate to:

From a parent on Reddit (r/personalfinance): "My 16-year-old made about $2,000 babysitting last year but it was all cash. Can she still open a Roth IRA?"

This comes up constantly. Yes, your teen can contribute to a Roth IRA if they have earned income, even if it's cash from babysitting or lawn mowing. The key is documentation — keeping a simple log of when they worked, for whom, and how much they earned. The IRS doesn't require a W-2, just legitimate earned income. Just make sure she can't contribute more than she actually earned ($2,000 earned = $2,000 max contribution).

From a Quora thread: "I tried explaining compound interest to my teenager and his eyes completely glazed over. How do I make this click?"

I felt this in my soul. Here's what worked for me: I stopped using percentages and started using real dollar amounts. Instead of "10% annual return," I said: "If you invest $1,000 today and leave it alone, it'll be about $2,000 in 7 years, $4,000 in 14 years, $8,000 in 21 years." The doubling pattern suddenly makes sense. Even better: let them use a compound interest calculator with their own money. When they see that their $500 birthday money could turn into $15,000 by retirement, something clicks.

Another parent (Reddit, r/FinancialPlanning): "Is it even worth having my kid invest such small amounts? She only makes like $100/month at her part-time job."

$100/month invested from age 15 to 65 at a 10% average return = over $1.3 million. That's $60,000 total invested, $1.24 million in growth. Small amounts matter enormously when you have time. The habit is more valuable than the dollar amount right now — you're teaching her a behavior she'll carry into adulthood when her income is much higher.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can my teenager actually open an investment account?

Sort of — they can't open a standard brokerage account on their own until 18, but you have options. A custodial brokerage account (UGMA/UTMA) lets you open an account in their name that you manage until they reach 18-21, depending on your state. Even better: if your teen has earned income from any job or self-employment, they can open a Roth IRA with you as the custodian. Fidelity, Charles Schwab, and Vanguard all offer custodial accounts with no minimums and no fees. I opened a Roth IRA for my 16-year-old using her babysitting income, and the whole process took about 20 minutes.

What if we lose all the money?

This is the most common fear, and it's worth addressing head-on. Can a single stock go to zero? Yes, absolutely — that's why we don't recommend individual stock picking for beginners. Can the entire S&P 500 go to zero? Only if literally every major American company simultaneously went bankrupt, which would mean we have much bigger problems than your investment account. The S&P 500 has never failed to recover from a crash, including the Great Depression, 2008, and COVID. The risk isn't losing everything — the risk is pulling out during a dip instead of staying invested.

How much should a teenager invest per month?

Whatever they can consistently maintain — even $10 or $25 per month. Seriously. The exact dollar amount matters less than building the habit of automatic, consistent investing. I'd rather see a teen invest $25 monthly for years than invest $500 once and never think about it again. A good starting point: suggest they invest 10-20% of whatever they earn. If they make $200/month at their part-time job, $20-40 into investments is reasonable and still leaves plenty for spending and saving.

Should they invest or save for college?

This depends on timeline. If college is 4+ years away, investing in a 529 plan or even a custodial brokerage could make sense. If college is 1-2 years away, that money should probably stay in a high-yield savings account — the market can drop significantly in the short term, and you don't want to be forced to sell at a loss right when tuition is due. My general rule: money you'll need within 5 years stays in savings. Money you won't touch for 10+ years can be invested.
