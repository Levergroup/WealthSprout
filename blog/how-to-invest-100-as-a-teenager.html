If You're 14–18, You're at the Exact Right Moment to Start Investing

Here's something I didn't know until I was 34: the best time to start investing is not when you have a full-time job. It's when you're a teenager with $100 and absolutely nothing to lose.

At 16, your brain is actually wired to take calculated risks. You're old enough to understand compound interest but young enough that a market dip doesn't wreck your rent payment. That combination is rare, and it disappears fast. By 25, most people are too scared to start. By 35, they're telling themselves they'll "get to it eventually."

My 15-year-old asked me last month, "Is $100 even worth investing?" I showed him the math: $100 invested at 16, earning an average 10% annual return, becomes roughly $3,800 by age 65. If he adds just $25 a month, that number jumps to over $260,000. He sat there staring at the calculator. Then he said, "Why doesn't anyone teach this in school?"

They don't. But I will. Right now, with $100 and a phone, you can start building real wealth. Not someday. Today.

What You're Actually Ready For (Even If You Think You're Not)

Let's clear up the biggest myth: you don't need to be a finance expert to invest $100. You need to understand three things, and most teenagers already get two of them.

First: what a stock actually is. A stock is a tiny piece of a company. When you buy one share of Apple, you own a microscopic fraction of Apple. Not enough to boss Tim Cook around, but enough that if Apple grows, your slice grows too. That's it. No magic.

Second: what an index fund is. Instead of buying one company and hoping it doesn't tank, an index fund buys hundreds of companies at once. The S&P 500 index fund — ticker symbol VOO or VTI — owns a piece of the 500 largest U.S. companies. When the overall market goes up, your fund goes up. When it goes down, your fund goes down. Over time, the market has historically trended upward.

According to Investopedia, the S&P 500 has delivered an average annual return of about 10% since 1926. That includes crashes, recessions, pandemics, and wars. It's not a guarantee, but it's the best long-term data we have.

Third: what compound interest means. This is the one that trips people up, but it's simple. Compound interest means your money earns money, and then that earned money earns more money. It's a snowball. The earlier you start rolling it, the bigger it gets.

The Next Gen Personal Finance (NGPF) reports that teens who start investing before age 18 develop stronger financial confidence and are significantly more likely to maintain investing habits into adulthood. The habit matters more than the amount.

With $100, you are not going to get rich tomorrow. But you are going to learn how the system works while the stakes are low. That's the whole point.

How to Start — Whether You're the Teen or the Parent Reading Along

If you're the teen: you need a custodial account. In the United States, you cannot open a standard brokerage account until you turn 18. A custodial account — usually a UTMA or UGMA — is opened by a parent or guardian but managed for your benefit. The money is legally yours, but the adult handles the transactions until you reach the age of majority (usually 18 or 21, depending on your state).

If you're the parent: this is your job, but it's also an opportunity. Opening the account together means you get to teach your kid how to research investments, place an order, and check performance. Don't just hand them the login and walk away. The account is the classroom.

Here's how to have the conversation:

Step 1: Show them the $100 → $3,800 math. Let them play with a compound interest calculator. The numbers do the teaching.

Step 2: Explain the difference between saving and investing. Savings accounts earn about 0.5% interest right now. The stock market averages 10%. Over 40 years, that's the difference between $122 and $4,500 on the same $100. Your kid needs to see that gap.

Step 3: Pick the account together. Walk through the app setup. Let them enter their own information (with you supervising). The more hands-on they are, the more ownership they feel.

The Exact Steps: How to Invest $100 as a Teenager

This is the part where I stop explaining and start giving you instructions you can follow right now.

Step 1: Pick a Custodial Investing App

You need a platform that offers custodial accounts with no minimum balance and low or zero fees. Here are the best options for teens in 2026:

  • Acorns Early: Best for beginners. Rounds up purchases and invests the spare change. $5/month for the family plan, but the automation teaches habit without requiring constant decision-making.
  • Fidelity Youth Account: Free. Real brokerage account for teens 13–17 with parental oversight. No minimums. Can buy fractional shares.
  • Greenlight Max or Infinity: Combines a debit card with investing. Parental controls built in. Good for younger teens who are just starting out.

Step 2: Fund the Account with $100

Link a bank account — usually a parent's checking account — and transfer $100. Some apps allow direct deposit from teen jobs or allowance apps.

Step 3: Choose Your First Investment

With $100, you want a single low-cost index fund or ETF. Here are the best beginner options:

  • VOO — Vanguard S&P 500 ETF. Tracks the 500 largest U.S. companies. Expense ratio: 0.03%.
  • VTI — Vanguard Total Stock Market ETF. Tracks the entire U.S. stock market. Expense ratio: 0.03%.
  • VT — Vanguard Total World Stock ETF. Adds international diversification. Expense ratio: 0.07%.

Buy fractional shares if needed — Fidelity and some other platforms let you buy $10 worth of VOO even if one share costs $400. You don't need to afford a whole share.

Step 4: Set Up Recurring Deposits (Even If It's Just $5)

The $100 is your starter. The real power comes from adding to it. Set up an automatic deposit of $5, $10, or $25 per month. According to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB), consistent small contributions build stronger savings behavior than occasional large lump sums.

Step 5: Check In Monthly, Not Daily

The stock market bounces around every day. That noise means nothing. Set a calendar reminder to check your account on the first of each month. Look at your total balance, not the daily change. If it's up, great. If it's down, remember: you're not touching this money for decades. A dip today is a discount for tomorrow.

The Best Tools and Resources for Teen Investors

Beyond the apps, here are the tools that actually help:

Compound Interest Calculator: Investor.gov has a free calculator run by the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission. Plug in $100, add $25/month, set the rate to 10%, and set the years to 49. Watch the number climb. That is the single most motivating tool you can show a teenager.

Investopedia Simulator: A free stock market simulator with $100,000 in fake money. Your kid can practice buying and selling without risking a single real dollar. Great for learning how orders work before you open a real account.

NGPF Arcade: The Next Gen Personal Finance arcade has games specifically designed for teens, including "Investing Basics" and "The Compound Interest Game." They feel like phone games but teach real concepts.

What People Get Wrong About Teen Investing

I have watched parents and teenagers make the same mistakes over and over. Here are the ones to avoid:

Mistake 1: Waiting Until You Have "Real Money"

The most common excuse I hear is, "I'll start when I have $1,000." That day never comes. $100 is enough to learn the mechanics, feel the emotional ride of market swings, and build the habit. The amount is not the point. The behavior is.

Mistake 2: Picking Individual Stocks Because They Sound Cool

Your kid wants to buy Tesla or NVIDIA because they like the products. That's fine with play money. With real money, individual stocks are gambling unless you know how to read a balance sheet — and most adults don't. Start with index funds. Get the basics right before you try to beat the market.

Mistake 3: Panic-Selling When the Market Drops

In 2022, the S&P 500 dropped about 19%. Teenagers who had just started investing watched their $100 turn into $81 and freaked out. The ones who sold locked in a loss. The ones who held were back to even within a year. The market rewards patience and punishes panic. This is the most important lesson your kid can learn with $100.

Mistake 4: Ignoring Fees

A 1% annual fee doesn't sound like much. But on a $100 balance with $25/month contributions over 40 years, a 1% fee costs you roughly $40,000 in lost growth. Look for platforms with zero commissions and funds with expense ratios under 0.10%. Every percentage point matters.

Mistake 5: Keeping It a Secret

Some teens hide their investing from parents because they think it's "their money" or they're embarrassed to ask questions. The best teen investors I know talk to their parents, their teachers, or at least a trusted adult about what they're learning. Money silence is how mistakes compound.

You don't need a five-year plan. You need a 24-hour plan. Here's what to do:

Today: Show this article to a parent or guardian. Pick one app from the list above and start the account opening process.

Tomorrow: Transfer $100. Buy one fractional share of VOO or VTI. Place the order together. Take a screenshot. That's your first investment.

This week: Set up a recurring deposit, even if it's just $5. Link it to allowance, birthday money, or a side hustle. Check out our teen side hustle ideas if you need cash flow ideas.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a teenager legally invest $100 in stocks?

Yes, through a custodial account (UTMA/UGMA) opened by a parent or guardian. The teen cannot open a standard brokerage account until age 18, but custodial accounts allow minors to own investments managed by an adult until they reach the age of majority. The money belongs to the teen, and the account converts to their full control at 18 or 21, depending on the state.

What is the best way to invest $100 as a teenager?

The best way is to open a custodial account with a zero-fee platform like Fidelity Youth Account or Acorns Early, then buy a low-cost index fund ETF such as VOO or VTI. These funds spread your $100 across hundreds of companies, minimizing risk while teaching the fundamentals of investing. Set up recurring deposits to build the habit.

Can you turn $100 into $1,000 by investing?

Not quickly, and not reliably. At a 10% average annual return, $100 takes about 24 years to reach $1,000. The real goal with a first $100 investment is learning how markets work and building a habit of regular contributions. If you add $25 per month, you can reach $1,000 in under three years — and much more over a lifetime.

What apps let teenagers invest money?

Fidelity Youth Account (free, ages 13–17), Acorns Early (automated round-ups, family plan), and Greenlight Max/Infinity (debit card plus investing) are the best options for teens in 2026. All require a parent or guardian to open the account and provide oversight.

Should a teenager invest in individual stocks or index funds?

For a first investment with $100, index funds are the better choice. Individual stocks carry higher risk and require research most teens (and adults) are not equipped to do. Index funds like VOO or VTI provide instant diversification across hundreds of companies, lower fees, and a smoother learning curve.

What happens to a custodial account when a teenager turns 18?

The account automatically converts to a regular brokerage account in the teenager's name alone. The parent or guardian loses oversight, and the teen gains full control. Before opening the account, both parent and teen should understand this transition and plan for it — including whether the teen is ready to manage the account independently.
