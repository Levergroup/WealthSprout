By Maya Hartwell

Key Takeaways

  • Kids 5-8 understand money best through concrete, hands-on experiences—not abstract conversations about saving
  • The 3-jar system works because it makes invisible concepts visible
  • Start with counting and sorting before jumping to earning or allowance

A Surprising Stat About Kids and Money

Here's something that stopped me cold when I read it: According to research from the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, money habits are formed by age 7. Not 17. Seven.

I didn't learn this until I was 34, staring at my own debt spreadsheet, wondering why I kept making the same financial mistakes. Turns out, my brain had cemented certain money patterns decades earlier—back when I was losing teeth and learning to read.

The flip side? Your kid between 5 and 8 is in the absolute sweet spot for money lessons. Their brain is a sponge right now. But—and this is the part that matters—only for the right kind of lessons.

Not lectures about saving. Not vague "money doesn't grow on trees" comments. The lessons that actually stick at this age are concrete, visual, and let them touch real money.

The Real Issue: What's Actually Missing

When my daughter was 6, she asked if we were rich. I fumbled through some answer about having enough. A week later, she told her friend we were poor because I said we couldn't buy a $40 craft kit.

That's when I realized: she had zero framework for understanding money. Not her fault—I hadn't given her one.

Most parents (myself included) assume kids pick up money concepts passively. We use our credit cards, talk about bills, maybe give them a few dollars here and there. But to a 5-8 year old, money is basically magic. It appears, it disappears, adults have mysterious rules about it.

What's actually missing isn't more piggy banks or apps. It's these three things:

1. Concrete cause-and-effect: At this age, your kid needs to physically see that when money goes into saving, it accumulates. When it goes to spending, it's gone. This seems obvious to us, but it's genuinely abstract to them until they experience it repeatedly.

2. Practice with real stakes (that are kid-sized): The difference between "I want that $8 toy but only have $5" is a perfect learning moment. The difference between "we can't afford the mortgage" is terrifying and teaches nothing useful.

3. Language for trade-offs: My 9-year-old now says things like "I'm choosing to save for the big Lego set instead of getting this small one." That language didn't happen by accident—we practiced it starting when he was 6.

Step-by-Step: Money Lessons That Stick for Ages 5–8

Here's what actually worked in our house, and what I saw work in my classroom before I left teaching:

Step 1: Start with Sorting and Counting (Week 1)

Grab your loose change. All of it. Dump it on the table.

Your job: teach them to sort by coin type and count. That's it. This isn't exciting, but it's foundational. Kids need to recognize coins and understand their values before anything else makes sense.

We made it a game. "How many pennies make a quarter?" "Can you make 50 cents using only these coins?"

Time investment: 10-15 minutes, twice a week for two weeks.

Step 2: Introduce the Three Jars (Week 2-3)

Get three clear jars. Label them: Spend, Save, Give.

This is the 3-jar system, and it works because it makes money visible and categorical. When my son got $6 from his grandma, we put $3 in spend, $2 in save, $1 in give. He could see his spending money was different from his saving money.

The ratios don't matter as much as the concept. Some families do 50/40/10. We did 50/30/20. Pick what feels right and stay consistent.

Step 3: Create Earning Opportunities (Week 3 onward)

Notice I said earning, not allowance. There's a whole debate about allowance-for-chores versus free allowance, and honestly, do what works for your family. But at this age, kids need to connect effort → money → buying power.

We started with: "You can earn $1 for washing the car with me" or "50 cents for sorting the recycling." Not regular chores (they still had those), but extra jobs.

The Next Gen Personal Finance research shows kids who earn money through effort develop better financial behaviors than kids who just receive it.

Check out our list of age-appropriate ways kids can earn money for more specific ideas.

Step 4: Let Them Buy Something (And Maybe Regret It)

This is where the learning happens. Your kid saves up $7, wants to buy a cheap toy at the store, you know it'll break in two days.

Let them buy it. Let it break. Talk about it.

"That toy didn't last very long, did it? What would you do differently next time?"

I didn't learn this lesson until I was 32 and bought a $400 handbag that I hated three months later. Your kid can learn it at 7 with $7.

Step 5: Introduce a Savings Goal

Around week 4-6, help them pick something specific to save for. Not "save your money"—that's too abstract. "Save $20 for that big Barbie house" is concrete.

We made a simple visual tracker (you can grab our printable kids savings goal tracker). Every time money went into the save jar, we colored in another section. My daughter checked that thing daily.

What Parents Get Wrong (I Did Too)

Mistake #1: Starting with allowance before they understand money

I gave my son a $5 weekly allowance when he was 5. It sat in his jar. He had no concept of what $5 could buy, what $20 looked like, or why waiting mattered. I was doing allowance because I thought I was supposed to, not because he was ready.

Better approach: Master counting and spending first. Allowance comes later.

Mistake #2: Protecting them from money disappointment

The first time my daughter saved up for something and then it sold out, I almost bought it on Amazon to avoid her tears. Thank god I didn't. She was disappointed for one afternoon, then picked a new goal. That's resilience.

If we smooth over every money frustration, they don't build the tolerance they'll need for bigger financial disappointments later.

Mistake #3: Making it too complicated

I tried a chore chart app with points and conversions and digital tracking. My kids were 5 and 7. It was too abstract. We went back to physical jars with actual coins, and suddenly they got it.

At this age, simpler is always better.

Mistake #4: Inconsistency

We'd do the jars for two weeks, then I'd forget for a month, then restart. Kids this age need repetition and routine. If you're doing allowance, same day every week. If you're doing earning, follow through on the payment immediately.

Quick Wins: Start This Week

If you're reading this thinking "this sounds great but I'm overwhelmed," here are three things you can do this week:

Quick Win #1: The Store Math Game

Next time you're grocery shopping, give your kid $3 in actual dollar bills. Tell them they can pick any snack that costs $3 or less, but they have to check the price tag and do the math. If they pick something that's $3.50, talk through why it won't work.

Time: 5 extra minutes at the store you're already going to.

Quick Win #2: The Choice Dinner Conversation

At dinner, describe a simple choice you made: "I wanted to buy the fancy coffee, but I chose the regular kind so I'd have money for our pizza night Friday."

You're modeling trade-off thinking out loud. My kids started doing this naturally within a few weeks.

Quick Win #3: Set Up One Savings Jar Tonight

Forget the full three-jar system if it feels like too much. Just start with one clear jar labeled with something they want. Put a photo of it on the jar. Every few days, ask if they want to add money to it. This builds the saving muscle.

Age-by-Age Breakdown: 5, 6, 7, and 8

What works at 8 will frustrate a 5-year-old. Here's what to expect at each age:

Age 5: Counting and Concrete Learning

What they can handle: Coin recognition, basic counting, understanding that money buys things

What to focus on: Sorting coins, counting to 20, visiting a store and watching a simple transaction

What's too much: Saving for long-term goals (their future thinking is about two weeks max), complex earning systems, percentages

Real example: My daughter at 5 could identify coins and count to 25. We practiced "giving" coins to me for small treats at home—a penny for a sticker, a nickel for a temporary tattoo. She learned that handing over money meant getting something in return.

Age 6: Beginning Trade-Off Thinking

What they can handle: Simple choices (this toy or that toy), saving for 2-4 weeks, understanding that money runs out

What to focus on: The 3-jar system, earning small amounts ($0.50-$1 per task), making purchase decisions with their own money

What's too much: Abstract concepts like interest, complex chore charts, waiting more than a month for a goal

Real example: At 6, my son earned quarters for helping with yard work. He saved up $4 over three weeks for a Hot Wheels set. The waiting was hard, but short enough that he maintained focus. When he finally bought it, the satisfaction was visible.

Age 7: Expanding Time Horizons

What they can handle: Saving for 1-2 months, understanding that some things cost way more than others, basic addition and subtraction with money

What to focus on: Bigger savings goals ($15-30), comparing prices, earning through more complex tasks, introducing the concept of giving/charity

What's too much: Percentage calculations they have to do themselves, managing money they can't see (fully digital), loans or credit concepts

Real example: My daughter at 7 saved for two months to buy a $22 craft set. She tracked her progress on a visual chart and could tell you she needed "$6 more." She also started noticing when things were "expensive" or "a good deal."

Age 8: Ready for More Complexity

What they can handle: Regular allowance, saving for 2-3 months, understanding wants vs. needs, beginning concepts of family budgeting

What to focus on: Weekly allowance with clear expectations, comparing quality vs. price, involving them in age-appropriate family money discussions ("we're saving for vacation, so we're eating at home more this month")

What's too much: Managing all their clothing money, complex investment concepts, full responsibility for major decisions

Real example: At 8, my son gets $4/week allowance. Half goes to saving, the rest he splits between spending and giving. He's saving for a $60 Lego set—it'll take him about 4 months. He checks his jar every few days and can calculate how much longer he needs to wait.

What Parents Are Actually Asking

I lurk in parenting forums more than I'd like to admit. Here are two questions I saw this week that I wanted to address:

From Reddit user throwaway_moneymom: "My 6yo daughter insists on buying these blind bag toys every time we go to Target. I've tried explaining they're a waste of money but she has a meltdown. I usually give in. How do I stop this cycle?"

Maya's answer: Stop buying them for her with your money—that's the cycle you're stuck in. Instead, let her save and buy them with her own money. Set up a small spending jar where she can earn $2-3 per week doing extra tasks. When you go to Target, she can check her jar first. If she has $5 and wants to spend it on blind bags, that's her choice. She'll either A) realize she doesn't want them as badly when it's her money, or B) buy a few, see they're disappointing, and learn from it. The key is removing yourself from being the decision-maker. You're not saying no anymore—her limited funds are. I did this exact thing with my son and those terrible surprise egg toys. Took three purchases before he decided they weren't worth it.

From Quora user Jennifer M.: "Is 5 too young to teach about giving/charity? My son doesn't even understand money yet and I want him to be generous but I don't want to confuse him."

Maya's answer: Five isn't too young, but keep it concrete. Don't start with "10% goes to charity"—that's abstract. Instead, when he gets $5, put $1 in a "giving jar" and tell him "this is money we'll use to help people." Every few months, let him participate in choosing where it goes. We took my daughter at 5 to drop her giving jar money in an animal shelter donation box. She could see where it went. That made it real. The generosity muscle builds the same way the saving muscle does—through repeated, concrete practice. But yes, master spending and saving first if he's still learning to count coins. You don't need all three jars on day one.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the best way to teach money lessons to 5-8 year olds?

The best method combines three elements: hands-on practice with physical money, clear visual systems like the 3-jar approach, and real (but small) stakes where they can make decisions and see consequences. Skip abstract lectures—this age learns by doing. Start with coin recognition and counting, then move to earning small amounts and making simple purchase decisions with their own money.

Should I give my 5-8 year old an allowance or make them earn money?

Either approach works, but at this age, connecting effort to money helps them understand cause-and-effect better. If you do allowance, consider making it regular and predictable (same amount, same day each week), but still offer opportunities to earn extra for additional tasks. The key is consistency—whichever method you choose, stick with it for at least 2-3 months before switching.

How much allowance is appropriate for kids ages 5-8?

A common guideline is $1 per year of age per week, but adjust based on your family's finances and what you expect them to cover. For a 6-year-old, $3-6/week is typical. What matters more than the amount is that it's enough for them to make meaningful choices (buy a small toy, save for a bigger one, or give some away) but not so much that they can buy everything immediately without thinking.

What if my kid wants to spend all their money on junk?

Let them—within the boundaries you've set. If they saved $6 and want to buy a cheap toy you know will break, that's a $6 lesson in quality vs. price. The mistake costs them, not you, and it's cheap tuition for a lesson they'd otherwise learn as adults with much higher stakes. After it breaks, have a no-judgment conversation: "That toy didn't last long. What would you do differently next time?" According to financial education research, kids who experience natural consequences of spending decisions develop better judgment than kids who are protected from mistakes.

How do I teach my child to save when they want everything now?

Start with very short savings goals—just 1-2 weeks for a 5-6 year old, up to 1-2 months for an 8-year-old. Use a visual tracker where they can color in progress, and keep the save jar visible so they can watch it grow. The key is making "future" feel concrete. Put a picture of what they're saving for on the jar. Count the money together weekly. Celebrate when they're halfway there. You're building delayed gratification like a muscle—you can't start with heavy weights.
