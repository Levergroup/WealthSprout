The single most important money concept a young child can learn is not saving. It is not budgeting. It is not even what money is. It is the difference between a need and a want — because every spending decision for the rest of their life will be a version of that question.

This free printable worksheet teaches the concept through sorting, drawing, and a short fill-in activity. It is designed for ages 5 to 10 and takes about 20 minutes to complete together. You do not need a finance background to use it. You need 20 minutes and a kitchen table.

What Are Needs and Wants — And Why Does It Matter

A need is something a person must have to be safe, healthy, and alive. Food, shelter, clothing, medicine, and education are needs. A want is something a person would enjoy having but could live without. Toys, treats, new shoes beyond what is needed, streaming services, and most of the things in a store's toy aisle are wants.

Both are okay. This is an important point to make with children — wanting things is normal and good. The skill is knowing the difference between the two. Because needs always come before wants when there is a limited amount of money — and money is always limited.

Most children who have not been taught this distinction believe that if they want something and a parent has a credit card, the money exists. Teaching the difference between needs and wants is, in part, teaching that money is finite and that choices have to be made.

"Wanting things is normal and good. The skill is knowing the difference — because needs always come first."

How to Use the Worksheet

The needs vs wants worksheet inside the WealthSprout Money Kit has three sections.

Section one is a sorting activity. Children are given 12 illustrated items — food, a home, a gaming device, medicine, candy, a new bike, school books, extra sneakers, water, a streaming service, a winter coat, a vacation — and asked to sort each into needs or wants. There are deliberate edge cases. A winter coat in a cold climate is a need. A fifth pair of sneakers is a want. These gray areas are where the best conversations happen.

Section two is a fill-in. Children write two things from their own life that are needs and two things that are wants. Then they answer: what would you buy first if you only had $5? This forces a real prioritization decision rather than an abstract one.

Section three is a drawing activity. Children draw their most important need and their favorite want, side by side. This visual comparison — what I must have vs what I love — is one of the most memorable ways to make the lesson stick.

The Conversation to Have While You Use It

The worksheet is a conversation guide more than a test. Here are the questions to ask as you go through it together.

"Is this a need or a want?" for each sorting item. Resist correcting immediately if they get it wrong — ask them to explain why they chose what they chose.

"What would happen if we didn't have this?" This is the clearest way for a young child to understand what a need actually means.

"What would you choose if you had $10 and could only buy one thing from your want list?" Forced choice between wants teaches prioritization, which is a foundational financial skill.

"Is there anything on the needs list we could spend less money on?" This introduces the idea that even needs have versions — a need for food does not mean the most expensive food.

Do not make this a lecture. It is a conversation with a printable in the middle to keep it tangible. Pair it with the 3-Jar System and the savings goal tracker for a complete first money education weekend.

Frequently Asked Questions

What age is best for the needs vs wants lesson?

Start as early as 5 or 6. At this age, the distinction is simple: food is a need, a new toy is a want. As children get older — 10, 12, 14 — the nuance increases: a phone might be a need for safety but a specific phone is a want. The early teaching creates the framework for those later conversations.

What do I do if my child says everything is a need?

Ask the question: what would happen if we didn't have this? If the answer is "nothing bad" — it is a want. This usually clicks quickly for young children once they apply the test themselves.

Is candy ever a need?

No. But food is a need. This distinction — the category vs the specific choice within the category — is one of the more useful financial thinking patterns a child can develop. Food is a need. A particular food is often a want.

How does this connect to saving and budgeting?

Needs come first in any budget. Teaching children early that the spend jar handles needs before wants gives them a mental hierarchy they can apply to any money decision later in life. For more strategies, see our guide on teaching kids about money.

The needs vs wants distinction sounds simple. It is. That is why it works. A child who truly understands it — who has internalized it as a decision-making tool rather than just a definition — will make better spending decisions for the rest of their life than most adults do.
