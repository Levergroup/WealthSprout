Free Kids Savings Jar Printable Templates That Make Saving Visible

Maya Hartwell Parent-Tested ✓

Former math teacher · Mom of two · Editor, WealthSprout

Key Takeaways

  • Physical savings jars work better than apps for kids ages 5–10 because they can actually see their money growing
  • Free printable labels turn any mason jar into a motivating savings tool in under 5 minutes
  • The best printables include progress trackers so kids can color in or check off milestones
  • You don't need fancy materials—a clear jar and regular printer paper work perfectly
  • Pairing a savings jar with a specific goal (like "New Lego Set" instead of just "Savings") doubles follow-through

Last Tuesday, my 9-year-old came home from his friend's house absolutely buzzing. Not about a new video game or some YouTube channel—he was excited about a savings jar.

His friend Max had this clear mason jar on his dresser with a homemade label that said "Max's Bike Fund - $150 Goal" and little boxes underneath where he'd colored in his progress. The jar was about halfway full of coins and crumpled dollar bills. My son stood there staring at it like it was treasure.

"Mom, can we make one of those tonight?"

Here's what I love about that moment: Max's parents didn't use some expensive piggy bank system or a complicated app. They printed a label, taped it to a jar, and suddenly saving money became visible. And for kids under 10, visible matters more than almost anything else.

I've made probably 15 different versions of savings jar printables over the past three years—some that worked brilliantly, some that ended up in the recycling bin after a week. I'm going to share the ones that actually stuck, plus the free templates you can download and print tonight.

Why Physical Jars Beat Digital For Young Kids

I'm a huge fan of teaching kids about digital money eventually. My 13-year-old has a Greenlight card and loves it. But for kids ages 5–10? Physical cash in a physical jar wins every single time.

When I was teaching middle school math, I learned that concrete representations matter for developing brains. Abstract concepts like "your balance increased by $5" don't trigger the same reward centers as watching five actual dollar bills go into a jar that's getting fuller.

There's research backing this up. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau specifically recommends using clear containers for young children learning to save because the visual feedback reinforces the behavior immediately.

My 9-year-old can't tell you his exact balance in his jar without counting, but he can tell you it's "way more full than last week." That feeling of progress? That's what builds the saving habit.

Plus, let's be honest—a jar sits on their dresser where they see it every single day. An app gets opened once every three weeks when you remember to check it together.

5 Free Printable Designs You Can Use Today

These are the templates I actually use with my own kids and have shared with hundreds of parents. All of them print on regular 8.5" x 11" paper and fit standard mason jars.

1. The Goal Tracker Jar

This is the one that started it all with Max's bike fund. The label has space for:

  • Your kid's name
  • What they're saving for (write it in!)
  • Their goal amount
  • A progress bar with 10 boxes to color in as they get closer

Why it works: Specific goals beat vague "savings" every time. My son saved $200 for a Nintendo game way faster than he ever saved for "the future."

2. The Three-Section Jar Labels

If you're using the three-jar system (Spend, Save, Share), this printable makes three matching labels so the jars look cohesive. Each has a simple icon—a piggy bank, a gift box, and a shopping bag.

Why it works: Visual consistency helps younger kids (especially ages 5–7) remember which jar is which without having to read yet.

3. The Milestone Sticker Chart Wrapper

This one wraps around the whole jar. It has spaces for 20 stickers going up the side of the jar—you add a sticker every time your kid adds money, regardless of the amount.

Why it works: It rewards the behavior of saving, not just the amount. My kid got just as excited adding 50 cents as adding $5 because either way, he got to put a sticker on the jar.

4. The Savings Thermometer

Like those fundraising thermometers you see at schools, but tiny and printed to tape onto a jar. You color it in as the money level rises.

Why it works: It creates a visual match between the actual money level and the tracker, which helps younger kids make the connection.

5. The "Days Until" Countdown

This label includes a calendar-style countdown. If your kid is saving for something happening on a specific date (birthday, holiday, field trip), they can cross off days while watching the jar fill.

Why it works: It connects saving to time, not just amounts. Some kids are more motivated by "20 days until I can buy it" than "$30 more to go."

How to Actually Make These (10-Minute Version)

You don't need a craft room or a laminator. Here's exactly what I do:

Materials:

  • One clear jar (mason jars from the dollar store, old pasta sauce jars, whatever)
  • Regular printer paper
  • Clear packing tape
  • Scissors
  • Markers or crayons if your kid wants to decorate

Steps:

  1. Download and print whichever template you want on regular paper
  2. Let your kid fill in their name and goal with markers (this ownership step matters—don't skip it)
  3. Cut out the label
  4. Tape it to the jar using clear packing tape (cover the whole label so it's water-resistant)
  5. Put some starter money in the jar immediately

That last step is crucial. An empty jar feels discouraging. I always put the first $1 or $5 in as a "matching contribution" so there's something to build on right away.

If you want to get fancy, you can laminate the labels or use a label maker, but honestly? Tape and paper have lasted us months without any problems.

What Parents Are Actually Asking

I spend a lot of time reading what parents are genuinely confused about. Here's what's coming up again and again:

"We've been doing the three-jar system but my 6-year-old keeps wanting to spend everything from the savings jar. How do I make saving feel more exciting than spending?"

— via Quora

Switch to a goal-based jar instead of a vague "savings" jar. When the jar says "Savings," it feels like a black hole where money disappears. When it says "Emma's Art Supplies Fund," suddenly there's a reason to put money in. Also, let them spend from it when they hit the goal—savings that never gets used doesn't feel rewarding.

"My kids count their money obsessively and I'm worried they're becoming materialistic. Should I hide the jars?"

— via Reddit r/parenting

They're not becoming materialistic—they're practicing counting and experiencing delayed gratification, which are both incredibly valuable skills. This phase usually lasts 2–3 weeks and then the novelty wears off. Definitely don't hide the jars; the whole point is that visibility.

"How much money should be going into the savings jar? We give $5 weekly allowance."

— via Quora

There's no perfect formula, but I like simple ratios kids can calculate themselves. With $5, we do $2 spend, $2 save, $1 share. With younger kids (ages 5–7), I'd even do $3 spend, $1 save, $1 share because they need that immediate spending satisfaction to stay engaged with the system.

The 3 Mistakes I Made With Our First Jar

Mistake #1: I made the goal too big.

Our first savings jar was for "College." My son was 6. He added $3, looked at the empty jar, and never touched it again. A 6-year-old can't conceptualize college, and even if he could, a $3 dent in $80,000 isn't motivating.

Now we do short-term goals only. The longest savings timeline I recommend for kids under 10 is three months. Any longer and they lose the connection between saving and getting.

Mistake #2: I didn't let him touch the money.

I was paranoid about lost coins, so I taped the jar shut and told him we'd open it when it was full. Terrible idea. Half the joy is physically putting money in, taking it out to count it, organizing it by coin type, and putting it back in.

Yes, you might lose a quarter. It's worth it.

Mistake #3: I picked the goal for him.

I thought "a bike" sounded more valuable than "Pokemon cards," so I pushed the bike. He saved $12 and quit. When I finally asked what he wanted to save for, he said a specific Pokemon tin that cost $25. He had that money in six weeks.

Your kid's goal might seem silly to you. Save your judgment. A $25 Pokemon tin taught my son more about delayed gratification than a theoretical bike ever would have.

What Comes After the Jar Gets Full

This is where it gets really fun. When your kid hits their goal, you have this amazing teachable moment.

Here's what I do:

  1. Celebrate like crazy. We take a photo of the full jar, they get to dump it out and count the total, and we make a big deal about them reaching their goal.
  2. Go buy the thing together. Let them hand over the cash themselves. The connection between "I saved this" and "now I get this" needs to be crystal clear and immediate.
  3. Ask if they want to start a new jar. About 70% of the time, my kids are excited to start saving for the next thing. The other 30% of the time, they need a break and that's fine too.
  4. Transition some kids to a savings account. If your kid is 8+ and has successfully filled 2–3 jars, they might be ready to understand a savings account at a real bank. The jar becomes the short-term fund, the account becomes the longer-term one.

My 13-year-old still has a jar on her dresser even though most of her money is digital now. She uses it for cash gifts from relatives and short-term goals (currently saving for concert tickets). The jar taught her the habit; now she applies it everywhere.

Frequently Asked Questions

What type of jar works best for kids savings?

Any clear jar works, but I love wide-mouth mason jars because kids can easily get their hands in to add or remove money. Pint-sized (16 oz) jars are perfect for younger kids (ages 5–7), and quart-sized (32 oz) work better for older kids (ages 8–10) who might be saving larger amounts. Avoid colored glass or ceramic containers—the whole point is that they can see the money accumulating.

Should I let my kid take money out of their savings jar?

Yes, but with structure. If it's a goal-based jar ("Lego Set Fund"), the money stays until they reach the goal. If it's a general savings jar, I let my kids withdraw money, but we talk about why first. Usually they realize they'd rather keep saving. The act of taking it out, counting it, and putting it back is actually part of the learning process.

How do I teach my kid to split money between multiple jars?

Start with the simplest possible system. For kids ages 5–7, I recommend just two jars: Spend and Save. At 7–8, add a Share jar. Use a visual ratio they can understand—with $5 allowance, lay out five $1 bills and physically show them "two go here, two go here, one goes here." After 3–4 weeks, most kids can do it themselves. Check out our guide on money printables for more tracking tools.

What if my child wants to save for something I think is a waste of money?

Let them save for it anyway (within reason—obviously not for something dangerous or inappropriate). My son once saved $30 for a toy that I knew would break in a week. It broke in four days. But you know what he learned? That sometimes things aren't worth the money, and now he asks me "do you think this will last?" before saving for stuff. That lesson cost $30 and was worth every penny.

How much money should a kid have saved by age 10?

There's no magic number, and honestly this is the wrong question. The goal isn't a specific dollar amount—it's building the habit of saving regularly. A kid who consistently puts $2/week into a jar and has saved $100 over a year has learned more than a kid who has $500 from birthday gifts sitting untouched. Focus on the behavior, not the balance.

Can I use these printables for older kids or teenagers?

The printable designs I shared here are really optimized for ages 5–10. Teenagers usually respond better to digital tracking and actual bank accounts. That said, some teens like having a physical cash jar for specific short-term goals. If your teen wants to use a jar, let them design their own label—the cutesy printables will probably feel babyish to them.
