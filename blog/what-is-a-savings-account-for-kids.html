What Is a Savings Account for Kids (And Why Most Parents Open the Wrong One)

Maya Hartwell Parent-Tested ✓

Former math teacher · Mom of two · Editor, WealthSprout

Kids who have a savings account in their name by age 8 are three times more likely to have savings habits as adults — and most parents don't open one until their kid is 10 or older. I didn't open one for my oldest until she was 7, and honestly, I wish I'd done it at 5. That single account changed how she thinks about money more than any conversation we ever had.

So here's the straightforward answer: a savings account for kids is a bank or credit union account held in a child's name (usually jointly with a parent) that earns interest on deposited money. But "what it is" isn't actually the interesting part. The interesting part is *which one* to open, what to do after you open it, and the moves that make it click for a 6-year-old versus a 12-year-old.

The Number That Stopped Me Cold

Only 55% of American kids have any kind of savings account — which means nearly half of children reach their teen years with zero firsthand experience managing money in a real financial institution. I didn't know that until I was deep into research for this site, and it genuinely surprised me. We hand kids an allowance, tell them to save, and then wonder why it doesn't stick.

Here's what the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau has found: kids develop their core money habits between ages 6 and 12. Not in college. Not in high school. Between first grade and middle school. That window is the whole game.

A savings account isn't a college fund or an investment — it's a practice arena. It's where your kid learns that money sitting still can grow, that a balance is something you watch and protect, and that patience has a literal dollar amount attached to it.

Why a Regular Bank Account Isn't Enough

Most people's instinct is to just add their kid to their own checking account or hand them a debit card. Fast, easy, done. But that's actually one of the least effective ways to build money awareness in a 7-year-old.

Kids' savings accounts are specifically designed with a few things a standard account doesn't have:

  • No monthly fees — standard accounts often charge $12–$15/month if the balance drops below a minimum, which will happen with a kid
  • No minimum balance requirements (or very low ones, like $1)
  • Joint ownership — you stay on the account as a custodian until they're 18, so you have visibility and can step in
  • Higher interest rates — some kids' accounts at online banks like Alliant Credit Union or Capital One offer 0.50%–3.00% APY vs. the national average of 0.46% at big banks
  • Educational tools — apps like Greenlight or accounts through Alliant show kids their balance in a visual, satisfying way

The psychological piece matters too. When your kid has an account in their name, it's theirs. That ownership changes how they treat the money inside it. My 9-year-old checks her Alliant balance the way I check my email — it's hers to watch.

If you're still working on the foundation of how to structure your kid's money before opening an account, our 3-jar system guide is the best place to start. It maps directly onto what a savings account does for the "save" jar.

How to Open a Kids Savings Account (Step-by-Step)

This takes about 20 minutes online. Here's exactly what to do.

Step 1: Choose the right account type. For ages 5–10, I recommend Alliant Credit Union's Kids Savings Account (0.55% APY, $5 minimum to open, no fees) or Capital One's Kids Savings Account (no minimum, no fees, easy app access). For ages 11–13 who want a debit card too, look at Greenlight ($5.99/month) or Chase First Banking (free with a Chase parent account).

Step 2: Gather what you need before you sit down.

  • Your government-issued ID (driver's license or passport)
  • Your Social Security Number
  • Your child's Social Security Number (yes, they need one)
  • Your child's date of birth
  • An initial deposit (usually $1–$25 depending on the bank)
  • Your existing bank account info to link for transfers

Step 3: Open it together, not for them. This is the part most parents skip, and it's the most important. Sit with your kid, show them the screen, explain what you're typing and why. "We're putting your name here because this account belongs to you." That five-minute conversation does more than six months of talking about savings in the abstract.

Step 4: Make the first deposit a ritual. Start with whatever they have — even $10 from birthday money. Let them see the confirmation. Let them screenshot the balance. Then set up a recurring transfer, even $5/week from their allowance. Automation removes the friction of remembering.

Step 5: Review it monthly, together. Pull up the balance on the first of each month. Show them the interest earned — even if it's $0.04. My daughter once got 11 cents in interest and acted like we'd struck gold. That reaction is the entire point.

What Parents Get Wrong About Kids Savings Accounts

I've talked to hundreds of parents through WealthSprout, and the same mistakes come up constantly. None of them are dumb mistakes — they're just the things nobody told us.

Mistake #1: Opening it and doing nothing else. An account with $50 sitting untouched teaches your kid nothing. The account is a tool — it only works if you use it consistently and talk about it.

Mistake #2: Choosing a big bank because it's familiar. Chase, Wells Fargo, and Bank of America all offer kids accounts — but their interest rates are often 0.01% APY. On $200, that's 2 cents a year. Credit unions and online banks reliably offer 10x–30x more. Investopedia's breakdown of kids savings accounts compares the top options side by side if you want to compare yourself.

Mistake #3: Treating it like a piggy bank. Some parents let kids withdraw freely whenever they want something. That's fine occasionally — but if every want gets funded by the savings account, it stops being a savings account. Have a simple rule: withdrawals need a 24-hour waiting period and a quick conversation about whether it's a "need it now" or "keep saving" situation. Not a lecture. A two-minute check-in.

Mistake #4: Waiting until they "understand money" to open one. A 5-year-old doesn't need to understand compound interest to benefit from having an account. They need to see the number go up. Understanding comes from the experience, not the other way around. Next Gen Personal Finance has solid research showing early account exposure is one of the strongest predictors of financial literacy in teens.

Quick Wins to Make the Account Actually Work

Opening the account is step one. Making it meaningful is the ongoing work — and it doesn't have to be complicated.

  • Match their deposits, even 50 cents on the dollar. If your kid puts in $5, you add $2.50. It gamifies saving and teaches matching (which is also exactly how a 401(k) works — hello, future).
  • Set a savings goal with a visual tracker. "I'm saving for a $40 Lego set" is 100x more motivating than "save money." Print a simple progress bar and tape it on the fridge.
  • Name the account something fun. Alliant and some other banks let you nickname accounts. My daughter's is called "Beach Trip Fund." She checks it way more than she would a generic "Savings" account.
  • Make interest visible on a whiteboard. At the end of each month, write the interest earned — even if it's tiny. Making invisible money visible is half the battle with kids under 10.
  • Connect their earning to their account. Birthdays, chores, lemonade stands — make it a habit that "money you earn goes here first." Our Money Moves hub has age-specific chore and earning ideas to keep the deposits flowing.

None of these take more than five minutes. The consistency of small, repeated moments is what actually builds the habit.

What Parents Are Actually Asking

These questions come up constantly in parenting forums, and both of these threads are worth a read if you're still on the fence.

"My 6-year-old just got $80 in birthday money. Should I open a savings account or just hold onto it? She keeps asking where her money is."

— u/tired_but_trying_mom on r/personalfinance

The answer from that thread — and from me — is open the account this week. The fact that she's asking where her money is? That's the exact instinct you want to reward with a real account she can see. Holding it "for her" in your own account is well-intentioned but it removes the experience entirely.

"Does it matter which bank I choose for my kid? Or is it basically all the same?"

— u/firsttimedadof3 on r/Parenting

It matters more than most people think — specifically because of the interest rate gap between big banks and credit unions, and because of the apps and tools that make the account engaging for kids. A 0.01% APY account at a big bank and a 2.5% APY account at Alliant are genuinely different experiences over three years. Choose intentionally.

FAQ

What age should a kid have a savings account?

Five is a great starting point — that's when most kids can grasp that money goes in, stays in, and grows. You don't need to wait until they "get" interest or banking. The experience of watching a balance grow is what teaches them, not a prior understanding of how it works.

Can I open a savings account for my child without their Social Security Number?

No — U.S. banks require a Social Security Number for any account holder, including minors. If your child doesn't have one yet, you'll need to apply through the Social Security Administration before opening the account. It's a straightforward process, just takes a few weeks to arrive.

What's the difference between a kids savings account and a custodial account?

A kids savings account is a joint account where you (the parent) remain a co-owner and the account converts to a standard account when your child turns 18. A custodial account (like a UGMA/UTMA) is an investment account that transfers full ownership to the child at 18 or 21 depending on the state — and it can hold stocks, not just cash. For ages 5–13 just learning the basics, start with the savings account. The custodial conversation comes later.

How much interest does a kids savings account earn?

It depends entirely on where you open it. Big banks like Chase or Bank of America typically offer 0.01% APY — that's 10 cents on $1,000 per year. Credit unions and online banks like Alliant (0.55%) or Marcus by Goldman Sachs offer dramatically more. Some high-yield accounts for kids push 3.00%+ APY. Always compare before you commit.

What happens to the account when my kid turns 18?

Most joint kids savings accounts automatically convert to a standard individual savings account in your child's name when they turn 18. Some banks require you to visit a branch or update the account type manually. Check your bank's specific policy when you open it — it's worth knowing upfront so there's no surprise when the time comes.
